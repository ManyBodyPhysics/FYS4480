\chapter{Many-body perturbation theory}
\label{chap:mbpt}
%=============================================================================

Chapter~\ref{chap:fci} showed that the exact ground state is a superposition
of all determinants, and that we cannot afford them.  Chapter~\ref{chap:hartrefock}
found the best single determinant, and Chapter~\ref{chap:mfapplications}
described the fluctuations about it.  Perturbation theory takes yet another
route: it accepts the mean-field determinant as a starting point and generates
corrections to it \emph{order by order} in the interaction, each order being a
finite, well-defined sum that can be written down and evaluated.

The appeal is obvious.  There is no matrix to diagonalise, no iteration to
converge, and no variational parameters -- only sums over intermediate states,
which for a two-body Hamiltonian are limited to particle-hole excitations of
low rank.  The price is equally clear and we shall be blunt about it: the
method is not variational, so nothing guarantees that a higher order is a
better answer; and the series need not converge at all.

This chapter develops the formalism algebraically.  There is a beautiful
diagrammatic language for organising these sums, and it is indispensable
beyond third order, but it is a separate subject and we leave it out here in
order to keep the algebra visible.  We derive the two classical schemes --
Brillouin-Wigner and Rayleigh-Schr\"odinger -- write out the first, second and
third-order terms, and then apply them to the four models that have run
through the book: the pairing model of Section~\ref{sec:pairingmodel}, the
Lipkin model of Section~\ref{sec:lipkin}, the Hubbard model of
Section~\ref{sec:hubbard}, and the pairing plus particle-hole model of
Section~\ref{sec:pairingphmodel}.  Every number is compared with the exact
answer and with the methods of the preceding chapters.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{An exact starting point}
\label{sec:exactstart}

\index{perturbation theory!many-body}
We are interested in the ground state, and we assume it is dominated by a
single determinant $\bket{\Phi_0}$ that solves an unperturbed problem,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{H}_0\bket{\Phi_0} = W_0\bket{\Phi_0},
  \qquad
  \hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \hat{H}_I .
  \label{eq:9-partition}
\end{equation}
The exact state is expanded in the eigenstates of $\hat{H}_0$,
\begin{equation}
  \bket{\Psi_0} = \bket{\Phi_0}
    + \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}C_m\bket{\Phi_m},
  \label{eq:9-expansion}
\end{equation}
with intermediate normalisation $\braket{\Phi_0}{\Psi_0}=1$, exactly as in
Section~\ref{sec:ciexpansion}.  Note that $\bket{\Psi_0}$ is therefore not
normalised to unity; this is a convenience, not an approximation.

Project the Schr\"odinger equation $\hat{H}\bket{\Psi_0}=E\bket{\Psi_0}$ onto
the reference,
\begin{equation}
  \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}}{\Psi_0} = E\braket{\Phi_0}{\Psi_0} = E,
  \label{eq:9-projectE}
\end{equation}
and subtract the corresponding statement for $\hat{H}_0$,
\begin{equation}
  \bracket{\Psi_0}{\hat{H}_0}{\Phi_0}
   = W_0\braket{\Psi_0}{\Phi_0} = W_0 .
  \label{eq:9-projectW}
\end{equation}
Both operators are Hermitian, so the two may be subtracted directly, giving
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \Delta E = E - W_0
   = \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Psi_0} .
  \;}
  \label{eq:9-exactDE}
\end{equation}
This is exact, and we call $\Delta E$ the correlation energy.  Every
perturbative derivation in this chapter starts from it.

As it stands, however, Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-exactDE}) is a rewriting of the
Schr\"odinger equation and nothing more: the right-hand side contains the
unknown exact state.  To make progress we must expand $\bket{\Psi_0}$ in
powers of $\hat{H}_I$.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Projection operators and the resolvent}
\label{sec:projection}

\index{projection operators}\index{resolvent}
Define the projector onto the reference and its complement,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{P} = \ketbra{\Phi_0}{\Phi_0},
  \qquad
  \hat{Q} = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\ketbra{\Phi_m}{\Phi_m}
          = 1 - \hat{P},
  \label{eq:9-PQ}
\end{equation}
so that our model space is one-dimensional.  Both are idempotent,
$\hat{P}^2=\hat{P}$ and $\hat{Q}^2=\hat{Q}$, both commute with $\hat{H}_0$ --
they are built from its eigenstates -- and $\hat{P}\hat{Q}=0$.  With
intermediate normalisation,
\begin{equation}
  \bket{\Psi_0} = \left(\hat{P}+\hat{Q}\right)\bket{\Psi_0}
   = \bket{\Phi_0} + \hat{Q}\bket{\Psi_0}.
  \label{eq:9-PQdecomposition}
\end{equation}

Now introduce an energy parameter $\omega$, to be fixed later, and rewrite the
Schr\"odinger equation by adding and subtracting
$\omega\bket{\Psi_0}$,
\begin{equation}
  \left(\omega-\hat{H}_0\right)\bket{\Psi_0}
   = \left(\omega-E+\hat{H}_I\right)\bket{\Psi_0}.
  \label{eq:9-omegaform}
\end{equation}
Provided the operator on the left has an inverse -- the \emph{resolvent}, or
unperturbed Green's function,
\begin{equation}
  \left(\omega-\hat{H}_0\right)^{-1}
   = \frac{1}{\omega-\hat{H}_0},
  \label{eq:9-resolvent}
\end{equation}
we may divide by it.  Multiplying from the left by $\hat{Q}$ and using
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Q}\frac{1}{\omega-\hat{H}_0}\hat{Q}
   = \hat{Q}\frac{1}{\omega-\hat{H}_0}
   = \frac{\hat{Q}}{\omega-\hat{H}_0},
  \label{eq:9-Qcommutes}
\end{equation}
which holds because $\hat{Q}$ commutes with $\hat{H}_0$ and is idempotent, we
obtain
\begin{equation}
  \bket{\Psi_0} = \bket{\Phi_0}
    + \frac{\hat{Q}}{\omega-\hat{H}_0}
      \left(\omega-E+\hat{H}_I\right)\bket{\Psi_0}.
  \label{eq:9-implicit}
\end{equation}

Equation~(\ref{eq:9-implicit}) is still exact and still useless: it is a
nonlinear equation for two unknowns, $E$ and $\bket{\Psi_0}$.  But it invites
iteration.  Substituting the whole right-hand side into itself repeatedly,
starting from the natural guess $\bket{\Psi_0}\to\bket{\Phi_0}$, gives
\begin{equation}
  \bket{\Psi_0} = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}
    \left\{\frac{\hat{Q}}{\omega-\hat{H}_0}
      \left(\omega-E+\hat{H}_I\right)\right\}^{i}\bket{\Phi_0},
  \label{eq:9-iterated}
\end{equation}
and, inserted into Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-exactDE}),
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}
    \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I
    \left\{\frac{\hat{Q}}{\omega-\hat{H}_0}
      \left(\omega-E+\hat{H}_I\right)\right\}^{i}}{\Phi_0}.
  \label{eq:9-generalseries}
\end{equation}
This is a genuine expansion in powers of $\hat{H}_I$.  Two things still spoil
it: the undetermined parameter $\omega$, and the appearance of the exact
energy $E$.  The two classical schemes are the two natural ways of disposing
of them.

\section{Brillouin-Wigner perturbation theory}
\label{sec:brillouinwigner}

\index{Brillouin-Wigner perturbation theory}
The first choice is $\omega=E$, which annihilates the awkward factor
$(\omega-E)$ and leaves
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E = \bracket{\Phi_0}{
    \hat{H}_I
    + \hat{H}_I\frac{\hat{Q}}{E-\hat{H}_0}\hat{H}_I
    + \hat{H}_I\frac{\hat{Q}}{E-\hat{H}_0}\hat{H}_I
      \frac{\hat{Q}}{E-\hat{H}_0}\hat{H}_I
    + \cdots}{\Phi_0}.
  \label{eq:9-bwseries}
\end{equation}
Every term is explicit and the structure is simple: alternating interactions
and resolvents.  The price is that $E$ appears in every denominator, so the
equation must be solved self-consistently -- guess $E$, evaluate the
right-hand side, update, repeat.

There is more to be said.  Write $\hat{e}=E-\hat{H}_0$ and recall that
$\hat{Q}$ commutes with $\hat{H}_0$ and is idempotent.  The geometric series
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Q}\frac{1}{\hat{e}-\hat{Q}\hat{H}_I\hat{Q}}
   = \hat{Q}\left[\frac{1}{\hat{e}}
     + \frac{1}{\hat{e}}\hat{Q}\hat{H}_I\hat{Q}\frac{1}{\hat{e}}
     + \frac{1}{\hat{e}}\hat{Q}\hat{H}_I\hat{Q}\frac{1}{\hat{e}}
       \hat{Q}\hat{H}_I\hat{Q}\frac{1}{\hat{e}}
     + \cdots\right]\hat{Q}
  \label{eq:9-geometric}
\end{equation}
resums Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-bwseries}) completely, giving the closed form
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \Delta E = \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I
    + \hat{H}_I\hat{Q}
      \frac{1}{E-\hat{H}_0-\hat{Q}\hat{H}_I\hat{Q}}
      \hat{Q}\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0}.
  \;}
  \label{eq:9-bwexact}
\end{equation}
This is not an approximation.  Solved self-consistently it returns the exact
ground-state energy, and the program \texttt{mbpt.py} verifies that it does,
for the pairing model, to eight digits at every coupling:

single small system, converge quickly.  They also have a fatal defect, which
Section~\ref{sec:sizeextensivity} exhibits.

\section{Rayleigh-Schr\"odinger perturbation theory}
\label{sec:rayleighschrodinger}

\index{Rayleigh-Schr\"odinger perturbation theory}
The second choice is $\omega=W_0$, the \emph{unperturbed} reference energy,
which removes the exact energy from the denominators at the cost of leaving
$\Delta E$ in the numerators,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E = \bracket{\Phi_0}{
    \hat{H}_I
    + \hat{H}_I\frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}
      \left(\hat{H}_I-\Delta E\right)
    + \hat{H}_I\frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}
      \left(\hat{H}_I-\Delta E\right)
      \frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}
      \left(\hat{H}_I-\Delta E\right)
    + \cdots}{\Phi_0}.
  \label{eq:9-rsseries}
\end{equation}
One simplification is immediate.  Since $\Delta E$ is a number and $\hat{Q}$
annihilates the reference,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Q}\,\Delta E\bket{\Phi_0} = \Delta E\,\hat{Q}\bket{\Phi_0} = 0,
  \label{eq:9-Qkills}
\end{equation}
so the rightmost $\Delta E$ in each term drops out and
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E = \bracket{\Phi_0}{
    \hat{H}_I
    + \hat{H}_I\frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}\hat{H}_I
    + \hat{H}_I\frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}
      \left(\hat{H}_I-\Delta E\right)
      \frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}\hat{H}_I
    + \cdots}{\Phi_0}.
  \label{eq:9-rsreduced}
\end{equation}

Equation~(\ref{eq:9-rsreduced}) is still implicit in $\Delta E$, and the
resolution is to expand it too.  Writing
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Delta E^{(i)},
  \label{eq:9-orderbyorder}
\end{equation}
and collecting powers of $\hat{H}_I$, we obtain the first three orders:
\begin{align}
  \Delta E^{(1)} &= \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0},
  \label{eq:9-E1}\\[2pt]
  \Delta E^{(2)} &= \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I
    \frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0},
  \label{eq:9-E2}\\[2pt]
  \Delta E^{(3)} &= \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I
      \frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}\hat{H}_I
      \frac{\hat{Q}}{W_0-\hat{H}_0}\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0}
    - \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0}
      \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I
      \frac{\hat{Q}}{\left(W_0-\hat{H}_0\right)^2}
      \hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0}.
  \label{eq:9-E3}
\end{align}
In a basis of unperturbed determinants, writing $V_{mn}$ for the matrix
elements of $\hat{H}_I$ and $D_m=W_0-W_m$ for the energy denominators, these
read
\begin{align}
  \Delta E^{(1)} &= V_{00},
  \label{eq:9-E1sum}\\
  \Delta E^{(2)} &= \sum_{m\ne0}\frac{V_{0m}V_{m0}}{D_m},
  \label{eq:9-E2sum}\\
  \Delta E^{(3)} &= \sum_{m,n\ne0}
      \frac{V_{0m}V_{mn}V_{n0}}{D_mD_n}
    - V_{00}\sum_{m\ne0}\frac{V_{0m}V_{m0}}{D_m^2}.
  \label{eq:9-E3sum}
\end{align}

The second term of Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-E3sum}) is the one to pay attention to.  It
is the price of having removed $E$ from the denominators, and it appears for
the first time at third order.  It is often called the \emph{renormalisation}
term, and it is not small: for the pairing model at $g=0.5$ the program finds
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{-0.02300347}_{\text{principal}}
  - \underbrace{\left(-0.01258681\right)}_{\text{renormalisation}}
  = \underbrace{-0.01041667}_{\Delta E^{(3)}},
  \label{eq:9-renormexample}
\end{equation}
so the correction is larger than the answer.  Its function, as
Section~\ref{sec:sizeextensivity} shows, is to cancel exactly those
contributions that would otherwise make the energy scale wrongly with the
size of the system.  This is the algebraic shadow of the linked-diagram
theorem.

\paragraph{Generating the series to any order.}
Rather than extend Eqs.~(\ref{eq:9-E1})--(\ref{eq:9-E3}) by hand, one uses the
standard recursion.  With $\hat{R}=\hat{Q}/(W_0-\hat{H}_0)$,
\begin{equation}
  \bket{\Psi^{(n)}} = \hat{R}\left[\hat{H}_I\bket{\Psi^{(n-1)}}
    - \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\Delta E^{(k)}\bket{\Psi^{(n-k)}}\right],
  \qquad
  \Delta E^{(n+1)} = \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Psi^{(n)}},
  \label{eq:9-recursion}
\end{equation}
starting from $\bket{\Psi^{(0)}}=\bket{\Phi_0}$.  Unwinding two steps
reproduces Eqs.~(\ref{eq:9-E2}) and~(\ref{eq:9-E3}) exactly, renormalisation
term included, and the program checks the agreement numerically for all four
models.  We use the recursion in Section~\ref{sec:convergence} to look at what
the series does at high order.

\section{The wave operator, and the link to configuration interaction}
\label{sec:waveoperator}

\index{correlation operator}\index{wave operator}
Before turning to numbers it is worth seeing what perturbation theory is
doing to the wave function, because it connects directly to
Chapter~\ref{chap:fci}.

Recall the correlation operator of Eq.~(\ref{eq:5-correlationoperator}),
\begin{equation}
  \bket{\Psi_0} = \left(1+\hat{C}\right)\bket{\Phi_0},
  \qquad
  \hat{C} = \sum_{ai}C_i^a\hat{a}^{\dagger}_a\hat{a}_i
    + \sum_{abij}C_{ij}^{ab}
      \hat{a}^{\dagger}_a\hat{a}^{\dagger}_b\hat{a}_j\hat{a}_i
    + \cdots
  \label{eq:9-correlationoperator}
\end{equation}
In a configuration-interaction calculation the coefficients come out of a
diagonalisation.  Perturbation theory produces them instead as an expansion,
and it is instructive to see the two agree at lowest order.

Take a Hartree-Fock basis, so that $\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_i^a}=0$
by Brillouin's theorem, Eq.~(\ref{eq:6-brillouin}).  A two-body interaction
can connect the reference only to $2p$-$2h$ states, so
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-E2}) collapses to the single well-known expression
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E^{(2)} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{abij}
    \frac{\langle ij\vert\hat{v}\vert ab\rangle
          \langle ab\vert\hat{v}\vert ij\rangle}
         {\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon_a-\varepsilon_b}.
  \label{eq:9-mp2}
\end{equation}
Compare this with the exact correlation energy in the same basis,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:5-energyeq}), which for a Hartree-Fock reference reduces to
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E = \sum_{abij}
    \langle ij\vert\hat{v}\vert ab\rangle\,C_{ij}^{ab}.
  \label{eq:9-fciDE}
\end{equation}
The two agree provided
\begin{equation}
  C_{ij}^{ab} = \frac{1}{4}
    \frac{\langle ab\vert\hat{v}\vert ij\rangle}
         {\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon_a-\varepsilon_b},
  \label{eq:9-firstordercoefficients}
\end{equation}
which is precisely the first-order perturbative estimate of the doubles
amplitudes.  Second-order perturbation theory is thus configuration
interaction with the doubles amplitudes taken to first order instead of being
solved for.

The difference matters.  Full configuration interaction determines
$C_{ij}^{ab}$ to \emph{all} orders in the interaction, whereas
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-firstordercoefficients}) is the leading term of an expansion.
Going to higher order in perturbation theory brings in successively more
complicated correlations -- and, at fourth order and beyond, genuinely new
excitation classes.  This is the sense in which the two hierarchies are
complementary: configuration interaction truncates the \emph{excitation
rank} and keeps all orders in the interaction; perturbation theory truncates
the \emph{order in the interaction} and keeps all excitation ranks that can
contribute.  Coupled-cluster theory, which we come to later, is designed to
keep the best of both.

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Choosing the partition.}  Nothing in the derivation fixed
$\hat{H}_0$; only that it be soluble and that $\bket{\Phi_0}$ be one of its
eigenstates.  Two choices are standard.  Taking $\hat{H}_0$ to be the
one-body part of $\hat{H}$ -- in a Hartree-Fock basis, the Fock operator --
is the M\o{}ller-Plesset partition, and it is what we use below.  Taking
$\hat{H}_0$ to be the entire \emph{diagonal} of $\hat{H}$ in the determinant
basis is the Epstein-Nesbet partition; then $\Delta E^{(1)}=0$ by
construction and the denominators contain the diagonal interaction as well.
The two sum to the same exact answer and disagree at every finite order.
Section~\ref{sec:partitions} compares them, and finds that neither is
uniformly better -- which is why a perturbative result quoted without its
partition means nothing.
\end{notebox}

\section{The pairing model}
\label{sec:mbptpairing}

We now apply all of this.  Take the pairing model of
Section~\ref{sec:pairingmodel} with four doubly degenerate levels and four
particles, $\xi=1$, and let $\hat{H}_0$ be the one-body part.  The reference
is the determinant filling the two lowest levels, with $W_0=2$, and the
first-order energy is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E^{(1)} = \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0} = -g,
  \label{eq:9-pairingE1}
\end{equation}
one factor $-g/2$ for each of the two occupied pairs.  The energy through
first order is therefore $2-g$, which is exactly the Hartree-Fock energy of
Section~\ref{sec:hfnothing}: for this interaction the mean field does
nothing, and first-order perturbation theory reproduces it.  Every subsequent
order is genuine correlation energy.

configuration interaction is variational, so it stays above the exact answer
at every coupling and degrades smoothly.  The random-phase approximation sums
a restricted class of contributions to all orders; it is the worst of the
three at $g=0.25$ and the best at $g=2$.

terms grow by ten and by twelve orders of magnitude between the tenth and the
fortieth, and the partial sums are worthless.  The radius of convergence lies
somewhere between $g=1$ and $g=2$; $g=1.5$ sits close to it, and no finite
number of terms settles the question there.

This deserves to be taken seriously rather than noted in passing, because of
how it looks from the inside.  At $g=1.5$ the first three orders are
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0.50000,
  \qquad
  \text{through second} = -0.15625,
  \qquad
  \text{through third} = -0.43750,
  \label{eq:9-innocuous}
\end{equation}
against an exact $-0.34819$.  Those numbers look entirely reasonable.
Nothing in them warns that the series behind them may not converge, and in a
realistic calculation one rarely has the exact answer to compare against.
Truncating a perturbation series at low order is an act of faith, and the
faith is sometimes misplaced.

The underlying reason is the same one we have met twice already.  The
expansion is about the reference determinant, and once the true ground state
has stopped resembling it, the expansion has nothing left to say.  In
Chapter~\ref{chap:hartrefock} the same coupling made the mean field unstable;
in Chapter~\ref{chap:fci} it made truncated configuration interaction
deteriorate.  Here it makes the series diverge.

\section{Size extensivity, and why Brillouin-Wigner is abandoned}
\label{sec:sizeextensivity}

\index{size extensivity}\index{linked-diagram theorem}
Section~\ref{sec:sizeconsistency} showed that truncated configuration
interaction fails a basic test: for two non-interacting subsystems it does not
give twice the energy of one.  We now apply the same test to the two
perturbation schemes, and the outcome is the sharpest distinction between
them.

Take $M$ identical, non-interacting copies of a small pairing system -- three
levels holding one pair, with the interaction acting only within a subsystem.
Any acceptable method must give exactly $M$ times the one-subsystem energy.
The program finds, for $M=2$:

Rayleigh-Schr\"odinger is exact \emph{at every order separately}, to machine
precision.  This is the linked-diagram theorem in its most concrete form.
The unlinked contributions -- terms in which the intermediate state contains
excitations in both subsystems with no interaction connecting them -- would
individually scale as $M^2$, and they are cancelled exactly by the
renormalisation term of Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-E3sum}) and by its analogues at higher
order.  That is what the term is for.

Brillouin-Wigner has no such term.  Its denominators contain the exact energy,
which is itself extensive, so the denominators of a two-subsystem calculation
differ from those of a one-subsystem calculation and the two can never match.
The error is $7.8\times10^{-3}$ at $g=0.5$ and grows to $4.7\times10^{-2}$ at
$g=1$, and it would grow linearly with the number of subsystems.  For a
calculation on bulk matter this is fatal, and it is the reason
Brillouin-Wigner theory, for all its formal elegance and rapid convergence on
small systems, is not used in many-body physics.

\section{The Lipkin model}
\label{sec:mbptlipkin}

The Lipkin model of Section~\ref{sec:lipkin}, with $W=0$, is instructive
because almost everything can be done in closed form.  The interaction
$(V/2)(\hat{J}_+^2+\hat{J}_-^2)$ changes $J_z$ by exactly two units, so
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E^{(1)} = \bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0} = 0
  \label{eq:9-lipkinE1}
\end{equation}
identically, and the reference energy is the unperturbed $-N\varepsilon/2$.
The reference couples to exactly one state, $\bket{J,-J+2}$, at an excitation
energy $2\varepsilon$, with matrix element
$\tfrac{V}{2}\sqrt{2N(N-1)}$, so
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E^{(2)} = -\frac{N(N-1)V^2}{4\varepsilon},
  \label{eq:9-lipkinE2}
\end{equation}
which the program confirms to ten digits.

The third order vanishes identically, and for a reason worth stating.  The
principal term of Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-E3sum}) requires a matrix element $V_{mn}$
between two states both reachable from the reference; but every such state
has $J_z=-J+2$, and $\hat{H}_I$ changes $J_z$ by two units, so $V_{mn}=0$.
The renormalisation term carries a factor $V_{00}=0$.  Hence
$\Delta E^{(3)}=0$ exactly.

$\omega=\varepsilon\sqrt{1-\chi^2}$ of Section~\ref{sec:rpastability} goes

imaginary and the method stops altogether.

but it does so silently.  It produces a perfectly ordinary number at the
coupling where the mean field becomes unstable, and would go on producing
ordinary numbers beyond it.  RPA is less accurate here and more informative:
its collapse is a diagnosis.  Whether one prefers a method that fails loudly
or one that fails quietly depends on whether an exact answer is available for
comparison, and in a real calculation it is not.

\section{Does the partition matter?}
\label{sec:partitions}

Nothing forced our choice of $\hat{H}_0$, and it is worth checking how much
the answer depends on it.  Taking the whole diagonal of $\hat{H}$ instead of
its one-body part gives the Epstein-Nesbet partition.

For three of our four models the two partitions give \emph{identical} results,
and the reason is worth seeing.  The diagonal of $\hat{H}_I$ is the same on
every determinant the reference can reach, so the two choices of $\hat{H}_0$
differ by a constant, which cancels in every energy denominator:

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch9summary}

Perturbation theory begins from the exact identity
$\Delta E=\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Psi_0}$ and turns it into a series by
iterating the resolvent equation~(\ref{eq:9-implicit}).  The free parameter
$\omega$ chooses the scheme.  Setting $\omega=E$ gives Brillouin-Wigner,
whose denominators contain the exact energy, which must therefore be found
self-consistently, and whose full resummation is exact.  Setting $\omega=W_0$
gives Rayleigh-Schr\"odinger, whose denominators are known in advance but
whose higher orders carry renormalisation terms.

Those renormalisation terms are the whole point.  They cancel the unlinked
contributions exactly, which makes Rayleigh-Schr\"odinger size extensive at
every order -- verified here to machine precision -- while Brillouin-Wigner
is not.  That single property decides the matter for many-body physics.

Applied to four models, the series behaves as its construction demands: very
accurate when the reference determinant is good, and worthless when it is
not.  It is not variational, so it may approach the exact answer from either
side and cross it; and it need not converge at all, as the pairing model
demonstrates beyond $g\approx1.5$, with no warning visible in the first three
orders.

What perturbation theory offers in exchange is a systematic, order-by-order
recipe requiring no diagonalisation, applicable where configuration
interaction is far out of reach, and -- crucially -- correct in its scaling
with system size.  The next step is to keep that property while summing
selected contributions to all orders, which is what coupled-cluster theory
does.

The accompanying notebook runs it cell by cell.

\subsection*{Exercise 1: the exact starting point}
\label{ex:9-exact}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 1: the exact starting point}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] Derive Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-exactDE}), stating where intermediate
  normalisation and the hermiticity of $\hat{H}$ and $\hat{H}_0$ are used.
  \item[b)] Show that $\hat{P}$ and $\hat{Q}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-PQ}) are
  idempotent, orthogonal and commute with $\hat{H}_0$, and prove
  Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-Qcommutes}).
  \item[c)] Obtain Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-implicit}) from
  Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-omegaform}), and explain why it is exact but useless as it
  stands.
  \item[d)] Why does the iteration~(\ref{eq:9-iterated}) start from
  $\bket{\Phi_0}$ rather than from any other determinant?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer to a).}
Projecting $\hat{H}\bket{\Psi_0}=E\bket{\Psi_0}$ onto $\bbra{\Phi_0}$ gives
$\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}}{\Psi_0}=E\braket{\Phi_0}{\Psi_0}$, and
intermediate normalisation sets the overlap to one, so the right-hand side is
$E$.  Similarly $\bracket{\Psi_0}{\hat{H}_0}{\Phi_0}=W_0$.  Since both
operators are Hermitian we may read the second as
$\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_0}{\Psi_0}=W_0$ and subtract:
$\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}-\hat{H}_0}{\Psi_0}=E-W_0$, which is
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-exactDE}).  Without hermiticity the two matrix elements could
not be identified and the derivation fails -- which is a real restriction for
non-Hermitian formulations such as coupled-cluster theory.

\paragraph{Answer to b).}
$\hat{P}^2=\ketbra{\Phi_0}{\Phi_0}\ketbra{\Phi_0}{\Phi_0}=\hat{P}$ by
normalisation, and $\hat{Q}^2=\hat{Q}$ by orthonormality of the $\Phi_m$;
$\hat{P}\hat{Q}=0$ since the reference is excluded from the sum.  Both are
built from eigenstates of $\hat{H}_0$, so
$\hat{H}_0\hat{Q}=\sum_m W_m\ketbra{\Phi_m}{\Phi_m}=\hat{Q}\hat{H}_0$.  Any
function of $\hat{H}_0$ is therefore also diagonal in the same basis, and
$\hat{Q}f(\hat{H}_0)\hat{Q}=\hat{Q}^2f(\hat{H}_0)=\hat{Q}f(\hat{H}_0)$, which
is Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-Qcommutes}).

\paragraph{Answer to c).}
Divide Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-omegaform}) by $(\omega-\hat{H}_0)$, multiply from the
left by $\hat{Q}$, use Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-Qcommutes}), and add
$\bket{\Phi_0}=\hat{P}\bket{\Psi_0}$ from
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-PQdecomposition}).  It is exact because no term has been
dropped.  It is useless because it is one equation in two unknowns,
$\bket{\Psi_0}$ and $E$, with the unknown state appearing on both sides.

\paragraph{Answer to d).}
Any starting vector would generate a formally correct series, but the
iteration converges usefully only if the starting vector already has a large
overlap with the target.  $\bket{\Phi_0}$ is chosen precisely because it is
assumed to dominate $\bket{\Psi_0}$ -- and when that assumption fails, as at
strong coupling in Section~\ref{sec:convergence}, the series fails with it.

\subsection*{Exercise 2: the two schemes}
\label{ex:9-schemes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 2: the two schemes}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] Set $\omega=E$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-generalseries}) and obtain the
  Brillouin-Wigner series~(\ref{eq:9-bwseries}).
  \item[b)] Derive the resummation~(\ref{eq:9-bwexact}) using the geometric
  series~(\ref{eq:9-geometric}), and explain why it is exact.
  \item[c)] Set $\omega=W_0$ and obtain Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-rsseries}); then show
  that Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-Qkills}) reduces it to
  Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-rsreduced}).
  \item[d)] Expand Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-rsreduced}) order by order and derive
  Eqs.~(\ref{eq:9-E1})--(\ref{eq:9-E3}).  At which order does the
  renormalisation term first appear, and why not earlier?
  \item[e)] Verify that the recursion~(\ref{eq:9-recursion}) reproduces
  $\Delta E^{(2)}$ and $\Delta E^{(3)}$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer to a).}
With $\omega=E$ the factor $(\omega-E+\hat{H}_I)$ collapses to $\hat{H}_I$,
and the $i$-th term of Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-generalseries}) becomes $i$ factors of
$\hat{H}_I\hat{Q}/(E-\hat{H}_0)$ acting on $\hat{H}_I\bket{\Phi_0}$, which is
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-bwseries}).

\paragraph{Answer to b).}
Identify the bracketed sum in Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-geometric}) as the expansion of
$(\hat{e}-\hat{Q}\hat{H}_I\hat{Q})^{-1}$ in powers of
$\hat{Q}\hat{H}_I\hat{Q}$, with $\hat{e}=E-\hat{H}_0$.  Substituting it into
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-bwseries}) collapses the whole series into
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-bwexact}).  It is exact because the geometric series has been
resummed, not truncated: no term of Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-bwseries}) has been
discarded.  Equivalently, Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-bwexact}) is what one gets by
solving the Schr\"odinger equation exactly in the $\hat{Q}$ space and
substituting the result back, which is a Feshbach projection and involves no
approximation at all.

\paragraph{Answer to c).}
With $\omega=W_0$ the factor becomes $(W_0-E+\hat{H}_I)=(\hat{H}_I-\Delta E)$
since $\Delta E=E-W_0$, giving Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-rsseries}).  The rightmost
factor always acts directly on $\bket{\Phi_0}$; there $\Delta E$ is a number
and $\hat{Q}\bket{\Phi_0}=0$, so that piece vanishes and each term loses its
final $\Delta E$, leaving Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-rsreduced}).

\paragraph{Answer to d).}
Count powers of $\hat{H}_I$, remembering that $\Delta E$ is itself of first
order and higher.  At order one only $\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0}$
survives.  At order two only the second term of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-rsreduced}) contributes, since the $\Delta E$ in the third
term would make it third order.  At order three the third term contributes
both with $\hat{H}_I$ in the middle -- the principal term -- and with
$-\Delta E^{(1)}$ in the middle, which gives
$-V_{00}\sum_m V_{0m}V_{m0}/D_m^2$.  The renormalisation term cannot appear
earlier because it needs at least two resolvents, hence at least three
interactions.

\paragraph{Answer to e).}
With $\bket{\Psi^{(0)}}=\bket{\Phi_0}$ the recursion gives
$\bket{\Psi^{(1)}}=\hat{R}\hat{H}_I\bket{\Phi_0}$, whence
$\Delta E^{(2)}=\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I\hat{R}\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_0}$, which
is Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-E2}).  At the next step
\begin{equation}
  \bket{\Psi^{(2)}} = \hat{R}\left[\hat{H}_I\hat{R}\hat{H}_I
    - \Delta E^{(1)}\hat{R}\hat{H}_I\right]\bket{\Phi_0},
  \label{eq:9-exe2}
\end{equation}
and projecting with $\bbra{\Phi_0}\hat{H}_I$ gives exactly the two terms of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-E3}), the second carrying $\hat{R}^2$ as required.  The
program checks the agreement numerically for all four models.

\subsection*{Exercise 3: the models to third order}
\label{ex:9-models}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 3: the models to third order}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] For the pairing model show that
  $\Delta E^{(1)}=-g$ and that the energy through first order is the
  Hartree-Fock energy of Section~\ref{sec:hfnothing}.
  \item[b)] For the pairing model with four levels and two pairs, evaluate
  $\Delta E^{(2)}$ by hand and compare with $-\tfrac{7}{24}g^2$, obtained in
  Exercise~\ref{ex:5-pairingfci}d).
  \item[c)] For the Lipkin model derive Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-lipkinE2}) and prove
  that $\Delta E^{(3)}=0$ identically.
  \item[d)] Explain why the Hubbard model at half filling has
  $\Delta E^{(3)}=0$ but $\Delta E^{(4)}\ne0$, and why this should not be
  expected in general.
  \item[e)] For the pairing plus particle-hole model, show that the
  $1p$-$1h$ part of $\Delta E^{(2)}$ vanishes in a Hartree-Fock basis.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer to a).}
The pairing interaction
$-\tfrac{g}{2}\sum_{pq}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p+}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p-}
\hat{a}_{q-}\hat{a}_{q+}$ has a diagonal contribution only from $p=q$, giving
$-\tfrac{g}{2}$ for each occupied pair.  The reference has two, so
$\Delta E^{(1)}=-g$ and $W_0+\Delta E^{(1)}=2-g$.
Section~\ref{sec:hfnothing} found the Hartree-Fock energy to be exactly the
reference energy, because $\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_i^a}=0$ by
seniority conservation.  The two agree, as they must: first-order
perturbation theory \emph{is} the mean-field energy when the reference is the
Hartree-Fock determinant.

\paragraph{Answer to b).}
Only the four seniority-zero determinants reachable by moving one pair
contribute.  Their unperturbed excitation energies relative to the reference
are $2,4,4,6$ and each matrix element is $-g/2$, so
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E^{(2)} = \frac{g^2}{4}
    \left(\frac{1}{-2}+\frac{1}{-4}+\frac{1}{-4}+\frac{1}{-6}\right)
   = -\frac{7}{24}g^2,
  \label{eq:9-exb3}
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~(\ref{eq:5-exb3}).  At $g=0.5$ this gives $-0.0729$, and the
program's numerical value is $-0.07291667$.

\paragraph{Answer to c).}
The reference is $\bket{J,-J}$ with $J=N/2$.  The operator
$(V/2)\hat{J}_+^2$ takes it to $\bket{J,-J+2}$ with matrix element
\begin{equation}
  \frac{V}{2}\sqrt{J(J+1)-(-J)(-J+1)}\,\sqrt{J(J+1)-(-J+1)(-J+2)}
   = \frac{V}{2}\sqrt{2N(N-1)},
  \label{eq:9-exc3}
\end{equation}
and the unperturbed excitation energy is $2\varepsilon$.  Hence
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E^{(2)}
   = -\frac{V^2\,2N(N-1)/4}{2\varepsilon}
   = -\frac{N(N-1)V^2}{4\varepsilon}.
  \label{eq:9-exc3b}
\end{equation}
For the third order, every intermediate state reachable from the reference
has $J_z=-J+2$; but $\hat{H}_I$ changes $J_z$ by exactly two units, so
$V_{mn}=0$ between any two of them and the principal term vanishes.  The
renormalisation term carries $V_{00}=0$.  Hence $\Delta E^{(3)}=0$ exactly,
and the vanishing is a consequence of the selection rule, not an accident of
the parameters.

\paragraph{Answer to d).}
The interaction is momentum-independent and couples only opposite spins, and
at half filling the ring is particle-hole symmetric.  Under these conditions
the third-order contributions cancel among themselves, and the program finds
$\Delta E^{(3)}$ to be zero to machine precision for every $U$.  The
cancellation is a property of this filling and this interaction: change
either -- or take the pairing model, where the third order is a perfectly
ordinary non-zero number -- and it disappears.  The fourth order is not
protected by the same argument and is not zero.

\paragraph{Answer to e).}
The $1p$-$1h$ part of $\Delta E^{(2)}$ is
$\sum_{ai}|\bracket{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}_I}{\Phi_i^a}|^2/D$, and by Brillouin's
theorem, Eq.~(\ref{eq:6-brillouin}), every numerator vanishes in the
Hartree-Fock basis.  In the unperturbed oscillator basis used in
Table~\ref{tab:mbptph} it does not, and the residue is a measure of how far
the reference is from the Hartree-Fock solution.  This is the practical
argument for building perturbation theory on a Hartree-Fock reference: it
removes an entire class of contributions at every order.

\subsection*{Exercise 4: size extensivity}
\label{ex:9-extensivity}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 4: size extensivity}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] For two non-interacting subsystems, show that the second-order
  energy is exactly additive.
  \item[b)] Show that the principal term of $\Delta E^{(3)}$ alone is
  \emph{not} additive, and that adding the renormalisation term makes it so.
  \item[c)] Explain why Brillouin-Wigner theory fails this test at every
  truncation order.
  \item[d)] Reproduce Table~\ref{tab:extensivity} and extend it to three and
  four subsystems.  How does the Brillouin-Wigner error scale?
  \item[e)] Relate all of this to the size-consistency failure of truncated
  configuration interaction in Section~\ref{sec:sizeconsistency}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer to a).}
With $\hat{H}=\hat{H}_A+\hat{H}_B$ and no coupling, the reference is a
product and any singly excited intermediate state lives entirely in $A$ or
entirely in $B$.  The sum in Eq.~(\ref{eq:9-E2sum}) therefore splits into two
disjoint sums, and the denominators of the $A$ states involve only $A$
excitation energies.  Hence $\Delta E^{(2)}(AB)=\Delta E^{(2)}(A)
+\Delta E^{(2)}(B)$.

\paragraph{Answer to b).}
At third order the intermediate states $m$ and $n$ may lie in different
subsystems: $m$ an excitation of $A$ and $n$ an excitation of $B$.  Such a
term is \emph{unlinked} -- no interaction connects the two -- and it
contributes $V_{00}$ times a product of $A$ and $B$ second-order-like
factors, which scales as the square of the system size.  Written out, these
are exactly the terms
$V_{00}\sum_m V_{0m}V_{m0}/D_m^2$, and the renormalisation term subtracts
them identically.  What survives is linked and additive.  The program
verifies additivity to machine precision through fourth order.

\paragraph{Answer to c).}
Brillouin-Wigner denominators contain the exact energy $E=W_0+\Delta E$, and
$\Delta E$ is extensive.  Doubling the system therefore changes every
denominator, so the two-subsystem sum cannot reduce to twice the
one-subsystem sum, at any truncation order.  There is no renormalisation term
available to repair this because the scheme has none: that was the whole
point of setting $\omega=E$.

\paragraph{Answer to d).}
The extension is a one-line change to the demonstration.  The
Brillouin-Wigner error grows roughly linearly with the number of subsystems
for weak coupling, so the \emph{energy per subsystem} drifts -- which is the
practical statement of the failure.  For a calculation on bulk matter,
where the number of subsystems is effectively infinite, no useful answer
survives.

\paragraph{Answer to e).}
They are the same defect wearing two hats.  Truncated configuration
interaction fails because a product of two double excitations is a
quadruple, which the truncation discards; Brillouin-Wigner fails because its
denominators do not factorise.  In both cases the symptom is an energy that
does not scale linearly with the system size, and in both cases the cure is
to make the ansatz multiplicative -- $e^{\hat{T}}$ instead of $1+\hat{C}$,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:5-ccsd}) -- which is coupled-cluster theory.  Rayleigh-Schr\"odinger
achieves the same scaling order by order through the cancellation of unlinked
terms, which is why it, and not Brillouin-Wigner, is the perturbative
starting point for many-body theory.

\subsection*{Exercise 5: numerical explorations}
\label{ex:9-numerical}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 5: numerical explorations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] Using \texttt{mbpt.py}, reproduce Table~\ref{tab:mbptpairing} and
  extend it to sixth order.  Does every additional order help?
  \item[b)] Reproduce Table~\ref{tab:convergence} and locate the radius of
  convergence of the pairing series as accurately as you can.  Why is this
  harder than it looks?
  \item[c)] Compare the two partitions of Section~\ref{sec:partitions} for
  the pairing plus particle-hole model over a range of $f/g$.  Is there a
  regime where one is clearly preferable?
  \item[d)] For the Hubbard model, verify that $\Delta E^{(3)}=0$ and check
  whether it survives a change of filling or the addition of a
  next-nearest-neighbour hopping.
  \item[e)] Implement a Pad\'e resummation of the pairing series and see
  whether it extends the useful range beyond the radius of convergence.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer.}
A few pointers.  In a) the answer is no: at $g=1$ the fourth-order term
overshoots and the fifth brings it back, the partial sums oscillating about
the exact value -- convergence is not monotone and no individual order is
guaranteed to improve matters.  In b) the difficulty is that near the radius
of convergence the terms decrease so slowly that any finite number of them is
consistent with either behaviour; the ratio test needs terms of very high
order, and those are contaminated by round-off once they have fallen many
orders of magnitude below the leading ones.  In c) neither partition wins
uniformly, but Epstein-Nesbet is the more stable at second order when the
diagonal interaction is large, which is the regime it was designed for.  In
d) the cancellation is fragile: it does not survive a filling that breaks
particle-hole symmetry.  In e) Pad\'e approximants do extend the useful
range, sometimes dramatically, because they can represent the singularity
that limits the Taylor series -- which is a reminder that a divergent series
is not necessarily a useless one.
