\chapter{Diffusion Monte Carlo}
\label{chap:dmc}
%=============================================================================

Chapter~\ref{chap:optimres} ended with a number and a complaint.  The number
was
\begin{equation*}
  E_{\mathrm{VMC}} = 3.000549 \pm 0.000049 ,
\end{equation*}
and the complaint was that the residual $5.5\times10^{-4}$ above Taut's exact
$3$ is eleven standard errors wide and entirely systematic: it is what a
two-parameter trial function cannot represent.  Optimising harder does not
touch it, and neither does sampling longer.  The only way past it, within the
variational framework, is a better guess -- and guessing is precisely what one
would like to stop doing.

This chapter stops doing it.  Diffusion Monte Carlo does not evaluate an
integral over a trial function; it \emph{projects} onto the ground state, by
propagating an arbitrary starting state in imaginary time and letting the
excited components die away.  The trial function does not disappear -- it
returns, importantly, as a \emph{guiding} function -- but it stops determining
the answer and starts determining only the efficiency.  For the two-electron
dot that is the difference between an energy accurate to $5\times10^{-4}$ and
one accurate to $10^{-4}$ and statistically consistent with the exact result.

The route runs through material already assembled.  The connection between
random walks and the diffusion equation is Section~\ref{sec:randomwalks}; the
Fokker-Planck equation, the Langevin equation and the drift are
Section~\ref{sec:fokkerplanck}; the quantum force, the Green's function and
the Metropolis-Hastings correction are Section~\ref{sec:vmcimportance}; the
optimised parameters are Eq.~(\ref{eq:14-final}); and the blocking analysis
that turns a correlated series into an honest error bar is
Section~\ref{sec:optresampling}.  Diffusion Monte Carlo is what those pieces
were being assembled for.

The programs are in \texttt{dmc.py}, which imports the trial function, the
local energy and the quantum force unchanged from \texttt{vmc.py} and the
resampling from \texttt{vmcoptimise.py}.

\section{Projection in imaginary time}
\label{sec:dmcprojection}

\index{diffusion Monte Carlo}\index{imaginary time}\index{projection}
Replace $t$ by $-i\tau$ in the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation.  The
oscillating phases $e^{-iE_nt}$ become decaying exponentials $e^{-E_n\tau}$,
and the equation becomes
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  -\frac{\partial \Phi(\bm{R},\tau)}{\partial \tau}
   = \left(\hat{H} - E_T\right)\Phi(\bm{R},\tau) \;}
  \label{eq:15-imaginarytime}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{R}=(\bm{r}_1,\dots,\bm{r}_N)$ collects all the coordinates and
$E_T$ is a constant energy offset whose purpose will become clear in a moment.
The formal solution is
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\bm{R},\tau)
   = e^{-\left(\hat{H}-E_T\right)\tau}\,\Phi(\bm{R},0).
  \label{eq:15-formalsolution}
\end{equation}

Now expand the starting state in the exact eigenstates of $\hat{H}$, which we
do not know but which exist,
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\bm{R},0) = \sum_{n} c_n \Psi_n(\bm{R}),
  \qquad \hat{H}\Psi_n = E_n\Psi_n ,
  \label{eq:15-expansion}
\end{equation}
and propagate term by term:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\bm{R},\tau)
   = \sum_n c_n\,e^{-(E_n-E_T)\tau}\,\Psi_n(\bm{R})
   = e^{-(E_0-E_T)\tau}
     \left[c_0\Psi_0 + \sum_{n>0}c_n e^{-(E_n-E_0)\tau}\Psi_n\right].
  \label{eq:15-filtering}
\end{equation}
Every excited component is suppressed relative to the ground state by
$e^{-(E_n-E_0)\tau}$, and since $E_n>E_0$ each of those factors goes to zero.
As $\tau\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\bm{R},\tau) \longrightarrow c_0\,e^{-(E_0-E_T)\tau}\,\Psi_0(\bm{R}) ,
  \label{eq:15-limit}
\end{equation}
whatever we started from, provided only that $c_0\ne0$ -- the starting state
must not be orthogonal to the ground state.

That is the entire principle.  It also explains $E_T$.  The surviving
prefactor $e^{-(E_0-E_T)\tau}$ makes the norm of $\Phi$ grow without bound if
$E_T>E_0$ and collapse to zero if $E_T<E_0$.  Tuning $E_T$ so that the norm
stays constant makes $E_T=E_0$: the offset that stabilises the propagation
\emph{is} the ground-state energy.  This is the \emph{growth estimator}, and
it is the first of two ways in which the energy will be measured.

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Why the excited states are the easy part.}  The convergence rate is
set by the gap $E_1-E_0$, and the amount of imaginary time needed is of order
$1/(E_1-E_0)$.  For the two-electron dot the gap is of order $\hbar\omega=1$,
so a few units of $\tau$ suffice.  What is \emph{not} controlled by the gap is
the starting point: if $c_0$ is very small, the ground-state signal stays
buried under the excited components for a long time.  Making $c_0$ close to
one from the outset is one of the jobs of the guiding function introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:dmcimportance}.
\end{notebox}

\section{A diffusion equation with a source}
\label{sec:dmcdiffusion}

\index{diffusion equation!and imaginary time}
Write $\hat{H}=\hat{T}+\hat{V}$ and put Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-imaginarytime}) in
coordinate representation.  In the atomic units of
Chapter~\ref{chap:applications}, where $\hbar=m=1$ and the kinetic operator is
$-\tfrac12\nabla^2$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \tau}
   = \underbrace{D\nabla^2\Phi}_{\textstyle\text{diffusion}}
   \;-\;\underbrace{\left(V(\bm{R})-E_T\right)\Phi}_{
     \textstyle\text{rate term}},
  \qquad D=\frac{1}{2}.
  \label{eq:15-diffusionsource}
\end{equation}
Two things are recognisable.  The first term is exactly the diffusion equation
of Section~\ref{sec:randomwalks}, Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-diffusion}), whose solution
we already know how to simulate: a random walk with Gaussian steps of variance
$2D\Delta\tau$.  The second is a first-order rate equation whose solution is
an exponential in $\tau$ with a position-dependent rate.

The diffusion coefficient $D=\tfrac12$ is not an adjustable property of a
physical fluid; it is the coefficient of the Laplacian in the kinetic energy.
That the imaginary-time Schr\"odinger equation looks like a diffusion equation
is not an analogy -- the two are the same equation with different letters.

\paragraph{The short-time Green's function.}
The two terms do not commute, so their effects cannot simply be applied one
after the other -- except over a short time, where the error is controlled.
This is the Trotter factorisation of Section~\ref{sec:qducc}: for small
$\Delta\tau$,
\begin{equation}
  e^{-(\hat{T}+\hat{V}-E_T)\Delta\tau}
   = e^{-\hat{T}\Delta\tau/2}\,e^{-(\hat{V}-E_T)\Delta\tau}\,
     e^{-\hat{T}\Delta\tau/2} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta\tau^3),
  \label{eq:15-trotter}
\end{equation}
which in coordinate space gives the \emph{short-time Green's function}
\begin{equation}
  G(\bm{R}'\!\leftarrow\!\bm{R},\Delta\tau)
   \simeq
   \underbrace{\frac{\exp\!\left[-\dfrac{(\bm{R}'-\bm{R})^2}
       {4D\Delta\tau}\right]}{(4\pi D\Delta\tau)^{3N/2}}}_{
       \textstyle G_{\mathrm{diff}}}
   \;\times\;
   \underbrace{\exp\!\left[-\Delta\tau
     \left(\tfrac12\left[V(\bm{R})+V(\bm{R}')\right]-E_T\right)\right]}_{
       \textstyle G_{\mathrm{branch}}} .
  \label{eq:15-shorttime}
\end{equation}
The symmetrised potential in the exponent is the trapezoidal rule for
$\int V\,d\tau$ along the step, and it is one order more accurate than
evaluating $V$ at either endpoint alone.

\paragraph{Walkers that are born and die.}
Equation~(\ref{eq:15-shorttime}) has an immediate stochastic reading.
Represent $\Phi$ by an ensemble of walkers, points $\bm{R}$ in the
configuration space, whose density is $\Phi$.  One time step then consists of

\begin{svgraybox}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Diffusion.}  Displace each walker by a Gaussian random vector of
  variance $2D\Delta\tau$ in each coordinate.  This is $G_{\mathrm{diff}}$,
  and it is the random walk of Section~\ref{sec:randomwalks}.
\item \textbf{Branching.}  Replace each walker by $m$ copies of itself, where
  $m$ is an integer with mean $G_{\mathrm{branch}}$.  A walker in a region of
  low potential multiplies; one in a region of high potential dies.
\end{enumerate}
\end{svgraybox}

\noindent
After many steps the density of walkers is $\Phi$, and the trial energy $E_T$
needed to hold the population steady is $E_0$.  There is no wave function
anywhere in the algorithm and no basis set: the ground state is represented by
where the walkers are.

\paragraph{It works, and then it does not.}
For a smooth, bounded potential this bare algorithm is perfectly usable.
\texttt{dmc.py} applies it to the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator,
$V=x^2/2$, with no trial function at all, and the population growth gives
\begin{equation*}
  E = 0.497322 \pm 0.002792 ,
\end{equation*}
against the exact $1/2$.  Nothing entered but free diffusion and branching.

Now put the quantum dot in.  The potential contains $1/r_{12}$, which is
unbounded, and the branching weight $\exp[-\Delta\tau(V-E_T)]$ therefore
diverges as two walkers approach each other.  A single configuration in which
the electrons happen to be close acquires an enormous weight, spawns an
enormous number of copies, and the entire population descends from it.  The
variance of the estimator is unbounded and no amount of computer time helps.
The same objection applies with more force to the Coulomb attraction in an
atom, where the potential is unbounded \emph{below} and the weights diverge
faster still.

This is not a technicality to be patched.  It is the reason why the algorithm
of this section, which is the algorithm as originally proposed, is never the
algorithm that is used.

\section{Importance sampling}
\label{sec:dmcimportance}

\index{importance sampling!in diffusion Monte Carlo}
\index{guiding function}
The cure is the one that has worked twice already.  In
Section~\ref{sec:mcintegration} importance sampling replaced a uniform
integration density by one shaped like the integrand; in
Section~\ref{sec:vmcimportance} it replaced a uniform proposal by one drifting
along the quantum force.  Here it replaces the propagated function itself.

Propagate not $\Phi$ but the product
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;f(\bm{R},\tau) = \Psi_T(\bm{R})\,\Phi(\bm{R},\tau)\;}
  \label{eq:15-mixeddistribution}
\end{equation}
where $\Psi_T$ is a fixed, known trial function -- from now on called the
\emph{guiding function}, because guiding is what it does.  Multiplying
Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-imaginarytime}) by $\Psi_T$ and rearranging, using
\begin{equation*}
  \Psi_T\nabla^2\Phi
   = \nabla^2 f - 2\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(\Phi\nabla\Psi_T\right)
   + \Phi\nabla^2\Psi_T ,
\end{equation*}
gives after a short calculation
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial \tau}
   = D\nabla^2 f
   - D\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(f\,\bm{F}(\bm{R})\right)
   - \left(E_L(\bm{R}) - E_T\right) f \;}
  \label{eq:15-importanceequation}
\end{equation}
with two quantities that are already familiar:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F}(\bm{R}) = \frac{2\nabla\Psi_T(\bm{R})}{\Psi_T(\bm{R})},
  \qquad
  E_L(\bm{R}) = \frac{\hat{H}\Psi_T(\bm{R})}{\Psi_T(\bm{R})} .
  \label{eq:15-driftandlocal}
\end{equation}
The first is the quantum force of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-quantumforce}); the second
is the local energy of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-localenergy}).  Both are already coded,
already differentiated by hand, and already checked against finite differences
in Chapter~\ref{chap:vmc}.

Compare Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-importanceequation}) with
Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-diffusionsource}).  Three things have changed, and all three
are improvements.

\begin{svgraybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{drift term} has appeared, and it is exactly the Fokker-Planck
  drift of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-fokkerplanck}).  Walkers are pushed towards regions
  where $\Psi_T$ is large, which is where the ground state has weight, instead
  of wandering.
\item The \textbf{source term contains $E_L$ instead of $V$}.  This is the
  decisive change.  For a trial function satisfying the cusp condition of
  Section~\ref{sec:vmctrial}, $E_L$ is finite everywhere -- that is what the
  cusp condition is \emph{for} -- and the branching weights are bounded.  In
  the limit where $\Psi_T$ is the exact ground state, $E_L$ is the constant
  $E_0$ and the branching switches off entirely.
\item The distribution being sampled is $f=\Psi_T\Phi$ rather than $\Phi$, and
  it is $f$, not $\Phi$, that the energy estimator wants.
\end{itemize}
\end{svgraybox}

\paragraph{The mixed estimator.}
\index{mixed estimator}
The last point deserves its own derivation, because it is what makes the
method cheap.  Once the projection has converged, $\Phi\to\Psi_0$ and
$f\to\Psi_T\Psi_0$.  Consider
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\displaystyle\int d\bm{R}\;\Psi_T(\bm{R})\,\hat{H}\Psi_0(\bm{R})}
       {\displaystyle\int d\bm{R}\;\Psi_T(\bm{R})\,\Psi_0(\bm{R})}
   = \frac{E_0\displaystyle\int d\bm{R}\;\Psi_T\Psi_0}
          {\displaystyle\int d\bm{R}\;\Psi_T\Psi_0}
   = E_0 ,
  \label{eq:15-mixednumerator}
\end{equation}
using only $\hat{H}\Psi_0=E_0\Psi_0$.  Now move $\hat{H}$ onto the other
factor, which is allowed because $\hat{H}$ is Hermitian, and insert
$\Psi_T/\Psi_T$:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  E_0
   = \frac{\displaystyle\int d\bm{R}\;\Psi_0\,\hat{H}\Psi_T}
          {\displaystyle\int d\bm{R}\;\Psi_T\Psi_0}
   = \frac{\displaystyle\int d\bm{R}\;f(\bm{R})\,E_L(\bm{R})}
          {\displaystyle\int d\bm{R}\;f(\bm{R})}
   = \meanb{E_L}_f \;}
  \label{eq:15-mixedestimator}
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{mixed estimator}.  It is the same average, of the same
function $E_L$, that variational Monte Carlo computed in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-localenergyaverage}); the only difference is the distribution
it is averaged over, $f=\Psi_T\Psi_0$ here against $|\Psi_T|^2$ there.  The
code that evaluates the local energy needs no modification whatsoever.

Two remarks are worth making.  First,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-mixedestimator}) is \emph{exact}: no assumption about the
quality of $\Psi_T$ was used, only that it is not orthogonal to $\Psi_0$.
Second, if $\Psi_T$ happened to be the exact ground state then $E_L$ would be
the constant $E_0$ and the estimator would have zero variance.  That does not
happen, but the closer $\Psi_T$ is, the smaller the variance -- the
zero-variance principle of Section~\ref{sec:vmclocalenergy} carries over
intact.

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{The mixed estimator is exact only for the energy.}  For an operator
$\hat{A}$ that does not commute with $\hat{H}$ the step taken in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-mixednumerator}) fails, and what the simulation returns is
$\bracket{\Psi_T}{\hat{A}}{\Psi_0}/\braket{\Psi_T}{\Psi_0}$ rather than
$\bracket{\Psi_0}{\hat{A}}{\Psi_0}$.  Writing $\Psi_T=\Psi_0+\delta$ and
expanding shows that the error is first order in $\delta$, and that the
combination
\begin{equation}
  \mean{\hat{A}}_{\mathrm{extrap}}
   = 2\mean{\hat{A}}_{\mathrm{mixed}} - \mean{\hat{A}}_{\mathrm{VMC}}
  \label{eq:15-extrapolated}
\end{equation}
removes that order, leaving an error $\mathcal{O}(\delta^2)$.  This
\emph{extrapolated estimator} is standard practice for densities, pair
correlation functions and anything else that is not the energy, and it is the
one place where a diffusion Monte Carlo result still depends visibly on the
quality of the guiding function.  The unbiased alternative, forward walking,
is considerably more expensive.
\end{notebox}

\section{The algorithm}
\label{sec:dmcalgorithm}

\index{diffusion Monte Carlo!algorithm}
The Green's function belonging to Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-importanceequation}) is,
over a short step, the drifting Gaussian of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-greensfunction})
multiplied by a branching factor built from $E_L$:
\begin{equation}
  G(\bm{R}'\!\leftarrow\!\bm{R},\Delta\tau)
   \simeq
     \frac{\exp\!\left[-\dfrac{\left(\bm{R}'-\bm{R}
       -D\Delta\tau\bm{F}(\bm{R})\right)^2}{4D\Delta\tau}\right]}
          {(4\pi D\Delta\tau)^{3N/2}}
   \times
     \exp\!\left[-\Delta\tau\left(
       \frac{E_L(\bm{R})+E_L(\bm{R}')}{2}-E_T\right)\right].
  \label{eq:15-fullgreens}
\end{equation}
One step of the simulation applies the two factors in turn.

\paragraph{Step 1: drift, diffuse, accept or reject.}
The first factor is precisely the proposal of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-proposal}),
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{R}' = \bm{R} + D\Delta\tau\,\bm{F}(\bm{R})
    + \bm{\xi}\sqrt{\Delta\tau},
\end{equation*}
applied one particle at a time exactly as in
Section~\ref{sec:vmcimportance}.  Crucially it is followed by the
Metropolis-Hastings accept/reject test of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-mhratio}), with the
Green's function ratio of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-greensratio}).

That accept/reject step was not part of the original algorithm and it is worth
being clear about what it does.  Without it the walkers sample the drifting
Gaussian, which is correct only to first order in $\Delta\tau$.  With it they
sample $|\Psi_T|^2$ exactly, whatever the time step, because the
Metropolis-Hastings test enforces detailed balance with respect to
$|\Psi_T|^2$.  The remaining time-step error then lives only in the branching
factor.  Numerically this is worth about an order of magnitude in the
time-step bias, which is why every modern implementation includes it.

\paragraph{Step 2: branch.}
The second factor is turned into an integer number of copies by the standard
device
\begin{equation}
  m = \left\lfloor w + u \right\rfloor,
  \qquad
  w = \exp\!\left[-\Delta\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}
    \left(\frac{E_L(\bm{R})+E_L(\bm{R}')}{2}-E_T\right)\right],
  \qquad u\sim U(0,1),
  \label{eq:15-branching}
\end{equation}
which gives $\mean{m}=w$ exactly while keeping $m$ an integer.  A walker with
$m=0$ is deleted; one with $m=2$ is duplicated, and the two copies then
diffuse independently and decorrelate.

\paragraph{The effective time step.}
\index{effective time step}
The $\Delta\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-branching}) is not a
misprint.  A move that the accept/reject test rejects means the walker did
not, in fact, diffuse for a full $\Delta\tau$, and reweighting it as though it
had is inconsistent.  Umrigar, Nightingale and Runge proposed measuring how
far the walkers actually moved,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}
   = \Delta\tau\,
     \frac{\sum_{\mathrm{accepted}}(\Delta \bm{R})^2}
          {\sum_{\mathrm{attempted}}(\Delta \bm{R})^2},
  \label{eq:15-efftimestep}
\end{equation}
and using that in the branching factor.  The ratio tends to one as
$\Delta\tau\to0$, so nothing is changed in the limit; at usable time steps it
removes a good part of the bias.  \texttt{dmc.py} implements both, and
Section~\ref{sec:dmctimestep} compares them.

\paragraph{Step 3: control the population.}
\index{population control}
Left alone, the number of walkers performs a random walk of its own and will
eventually hit zero or exhaust memory.  It is stabilised by feeding the
population back into the trial energy,
\begin{equation}
  E_T \;=\; \bar{E} \;-\; \frac{\kappa}{\Delta\tau}
    \ln\frac{N_{\mathrm{walkers}}}{N_{\mathrm{target}}},
  \label{eq:15-populationcontrol}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{E}$ is a running average of the mixed estimator.  When the
population grows, $E_T$ drops and the branching weights fall below one; when
it shrinks, the reverse.  The feedback strength $\kappa$ must be small --
\texttt{dmc.py} uses $0.05$ -- because $E_T$ appears in the branching factor,
and a violent controller injects its own fluctuations into the simulation.

The price of the feedback is a \emph{population-control bias}: the walkers are
no longer independent, since the fate of one affects $E_T$ and hence the fate
of all the others.  The bias is $\mathcal{O}(1/N_{\mathrm{walkers}})$, and it
is checked by varying $N_{\mathrm{walkers}}$, which
Section~\ref{sec:dmcresults} does.

\paragraph{Step 4: measure.}
The mixed estimator is the average of $E_L$ over the current population,
recorded once per step.  That series is strongly autocorrelated -- more so
than in Chapter~\ref{chap:optimres}, because branching correlates a walker
with its parent as well as with its own past -- so the error bar comes from
blocking, exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:optresampling}.  The growth estimator
of Section~\ref{sec:dmcprojection} is the average of $E_T$ over the same
series, and it is a useful independent check.

\paragraph{Starting the walkers.}
The ensemble is initialised by a short variational Monte Carlo run at the same
$\alpha$ and $\beta$.  This is not merely convenient.  A good guiding function
makes $|\Psi_T|^2$ close to $f=\Psi_T\Psi_0$ already, so the projection has
little left to do and the equilibration phase is short -- which is exactly the
statement that $c_0$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-expansion}) is close to one.

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{What the guiding function is now for.}  In Chapter~\ref{chap:vmc}
$\Psi_T$ \emph{was} the answer, and its deficiencies appeared directly in the
energy.  Here it does four separate jobs, none of which is to determine the
result: it supplies the drift that steers the walkers; it replaces $V$ by
$E_L$ in the branching, which is what makes the weights finite; it defines the
mixed estimator; and it fixes the nodes, in the fermionic case of
Section~\ref{sec:dmcfixednode}.  Only the last of these biases the energy.
For a nodeless ground state the guiding function affects the error bar and the
time-step sensitivity, and nothing else.
\end{notebox}

\section{Fermions: the sign problem and the fixed-node approximation}
\label{sec:dmcfixednode}

\index{sign problem}\index{fixed-node approximation}
Everything so far assumed that $\Phi$ can be read as a density of walkers,
which requires it to be positive.  For a bosonic ground state that is
automatic: the lowest solution of Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-imaginarytime}) is nodeless.
For fermions it is false, and the failure is fundamental rather than
technical.

An antisymmetric wave function changes sign, so it has nodes -- a surface of
one dimension less than the configuration space, on which it vanishes.
Meanwhile the imaginary-time propagator projects onto the \emph{lowest} state
of the Hamiltonian irrespective of symmetry, and that state is the nodeless
bosonic one, which lies below the fermionic ground state.  Propagating long
enough therefore destroys exactly the state we want:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\text{fermionic signal}}{\text{bosonic noise}}
   \;\sim\; e^{-\left(E_0^{\mathrm{F}}-E_0^{\mathrm{B}}\right)\tau},
  \label{eq:15-signproblem}
\end{equation}
which decays exponentially in $\tau$ and, at fixed statistical accuracy,
exponentially in the number of particles.  This is the \emph{fermion sign
problem}.  It is the same obstruction, in different dress, that made the exact
diagonalisation of Chapter~\ref{chap:fci} scale factorially, and no general
solution is known.

The practical response is the \emph{fixed-node approximation}.  Impose on the
projected state the nodes of the guiding function: require
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\bm{R}) = 0 \quad\text{wherever}\quad \Psi_T(\bm{R}) = 0 ,
  \label{eq:15-fixednode}
\end{equation}
so that $f=\Psi_T\Phi\ge0$ everywhere and the walker interpretation survives.
In the algorithm this costs one line: a move that would carry a walker across
a node, changing the sign of $\Psi_T$, is rejected.  The nodal surface then
partitions configuration space into disjoint pockets, and the simulation
solves the Schr\"odinger equation exactly inside each pocket, with the
boundary condition $\Phi=0$ on the walls.

What this buys is a theorem rather than an approximation of unknown quality.
The fixed-node energy is a \emph{variational upper bound} to the exact
fermionic ground-state energy,
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{FN}} \;\ge\; E_0^{\mathrm{F}},
  \label{eq:15-fixednodebound}
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if the nodes of $\Psi_T$ are the exact ones.  The
argument is the variational principle of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-variational}) applied
within the class of functions vanishing on the imposed surface.  The hierarchy
is therefore clear: the fixed-node energy is bounded below by the truth and
above by the variational energy of the same trial function, and improving the
\emph{nodes} -- not the amplitude -- is the only way to tighten it.

\begin{svgraybox}
For the system of this chapter none of this applies.  Two electrons in a
singlet have an antisymmetric spin function, so the spatial part is symmetric,
and the trial function
\begin{equation*}
  \Psi_T(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2)
   = e^{-\alpha\omega(r_1^2+r_2^2)/2}\,
     e^{r_{12}/(1+\beta r_{12})}
\end{equation*}
of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-trial}) is strictly positive: nodeless.  The projection is
exact in principle, and the only errors left are the time step, the finite
population and the statistics -- all three of which can be measured and
reduced.  This is what makes the two-electron dot the right place to see what
diffusion Monte Carlo actually does, uncontaminated by the one approximation
that cannot be checked.
\end{svgraybox}

\section{The time step}
\label{sec:dmctimestep}

\index{time step error}
The short-time Green's function, Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-fullgreens}), is exact only
as $\Delta\tau\to0$.  Every diffusion Monte Carlo energy therefore carries a
systematic error in $\Delta\tau$, and a calculation that quotes one time step
has not finished.  The procedure is to run at several and extrapolate.

Three observations, in increasing order of interest.

The acceptance rate stays above $87\%$ even at $\Delta\tau=0.5$, which is what
the drift term buys and is unchanged from Chapter~\ref{chap:vmc}.  With
$\Delta\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}$ switched on, the correction it applies is
substantial at the largest time step -- the walkers move only $80\%$ of the
distance they attempt -- and negligible below $\Delta\tau=0.05$, exactly as it
should be.

The correlation time in \emph{steps} grows as the time step shrinks: $1.2$ at
$\Delta\tau=0.5$ against $16$ at $\Delta\tau=0.02$.  This is not a defect but
arithmetic.  What decorrelates a walker is imaginary time, and $4000$ steps at
$\Delta\tau=0.02$ cover eighty units of $\tau$ where the same number of steps
at $\Delta\tau=0.5$ cover two thousand.  Halving the time step therefore
doubles the number of steps needed for the same statistics, which is why the
error bars in the table grow downwards even though the number of samples is
constant.  A small time step is not free.

And the bias itself is minute -- a few parts in $10^{4}$ across a factor of
twenty-five in $\Delta\tau$.  That is a statement about the guiding function,
not about the method.  The time-step error originates in the commutators
dropped by the Trotter factorisation, Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-trotter}), and those
vanish when $\Psi_T$ is an eigenfunction.  At $\alpha=1$ the Gaussian part of
the trial function is exact, the Jastrow factor satisfies the cusp condition,
and the local energy is very nearly constant, so there is almost nothing left
for the factorisation to get wrong.  The next section shows what happens when
that is not so.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:dmcresults}

\paragraph{The chapter's number.}
A single run at $\Delta\tau=0.05$ with $500$ walkers gives, in about two
seconds,
\begin{align*}
  \text{mixed estimator} \quad&E = 3.000216 \pm 0.000115,\\
  \text{growth estimator}\quad&E = 2.999778 \pm 0.000619,
\end{align*}
with a variance of the local energy over the population of $0.0022$.  The two
agree, as they must -- one measures the eigenvalue directly, the other the
rate at which the population would grow if $E_T$ were frozen -- and the mixed
estimator has five times the smaller error, which is why it is the one
quoted.  Extrapolating to zero time step from Table~\ref{tab:15-timestep}
gives the result of the chapter,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;E_{\mathrm{DMC}} = 3.000090 \pm 0.000064\;}
  \label{eq:15-final}
\end{equation}
which sits $1.4$ standard errors above the exact $3$.

performed from $\Delta\tau\le0.1$.  A poor guiding function costs computer
time and demands a smaller time step.  It does not cost accuracy.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:dmcsummary}

Imaginary-time propagation filters out excited states exponentially,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-filtering}), and the resulting equation for the ground state
is a diffusion equation with a source, Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-diffusionsource}),
which a branching random walk simulates directly.  In that bare form the
branching weights diverge for any singular potential and the method is
unusable for electrons.  Importance sampling with a guiding function cures it,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-importanceequation}): the drift becomes the quantum force of
Chapter~\ref{chap:vmc}, the source becomes the local energy, which the cusp
condition keeps finite, and the ground-state energy is the mixed estimator
$\meanb{E_L}_f$ -- the same average of the same function as in variational
Monte Carlo, taken over a different distribution.

The remaining errors are of four kinds, and it matters that they differ in
character.  The statistical error falls as $1/\sqrt{N}$ and is measured by
blocking.  The time-step error is systematic, is removed by extrapolation, and
is small when the guiding function is good.  The population-control bias falls
as $1/N_{\mathrm{walkers}}$ and is checked by varying it.  Only the fourth,
the fixed-node error, cannot be removed by spending more computer time, and it
is absent here because the two-electron singlet is nodeless.  For real
fermionic systems it is the dominant error, and reducing it means improving
the \emph{nodes} of the trial function.

Which points at what is missing.  Everything from Chapter~\ref{chap:vmc}
onwards has rested on writing down a functional form for $\Psi_T$ and hoping
it is close enough -- close enough for a useful variational bound in
Chapter~\ref{chap:vmc}, close enough for a small time-step bias here, and in
the fermionic case close enough in its \emph{nodes}, which is a far more
delicate requirement than being close in amplitude.  The Pad\'e-Jastrow form
has two parameters and was chosen by hand from physical arguments about cusps.
The alternative is not to choose: to let $\Psi_T$ be a flexible parametrised
function -- a neural network -- and to fix its thousands of parameters with
the gradient of Eq.~(\ref{eq:14-gradient}) and the stochastic reconfiguration
of Eq.~(\ref{eq:14-sr}), both of which were written for an arbitrary number of
parameters precisely so that they would still apply.  That is the subject of
the chapters that follow.

\subsection*{Exercise 1: the importance-sampled equation}
\label{ex:15-derivation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 1: the importance-sampled equation}

\begin{enumerate}
\item[a)] Starting from Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-imaginarytime}) with
  $\hat{H}=-D\nabla^2+V$, multiply by $\Psi_T(\bm{R})$ and use the identity
  \begin{equation*}
    \Psi_T\nabla^2\Phi
     = \nabla^2 f - 2\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(\Phi\nabla\Psi_T\right)
     + \Phi\nabla^2\Psi_T
  \end{equation*}
  to derive Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-importanceequation}).  Identify the step at which
  the potential $V$ turns into the local energy $E_L$.

\item[b)] Verify that if $\Psi_T$ is the exact ground state then the source
  term in Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-importanceequation}) vanishes identically when
  $E_T=E_0$.  What does the algorithm reduce to in that case, and what is the
  variance of the energy estimator?

\item[c)] Show that the stationary solution of
  Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-importanceequation}) with the source term removed is
  $f\propto|\Psi_T|^2$, and explain why this is the right check that the
  drift-diffusion part of the algorithm is the variational Monte Carlo of
  Chapter~\ref{chap:vmc}.

\item[d)] Derive the extrapolated estimator, Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-extrapolated}),
  by writing $\Psi_T=\Psi_0+\delta$, expanding both
  $\mean{\hat{A}}_{\mathrm{mixed}}$ and $\mean{\hat{A}}_{\mathrm{VMC}}$ to
  first order in $\delta$, and eliminating that order.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exercise 2: branching and the population}
\label{ex:15-branching}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 2: branching and the population}

\begin{enumerate}
\item[a)] Show that $m=\lfloor w+u\rfloor$ with $u$ uniform on $[0,1)$ has
  mean exactly $w$, and compute its variance.  Why is this preferable to
  rounding $w$ to the nearest integer?

\item[b)] \texttt{dmc.py} clips $m$ at $3$.  Explain what this protects
  against, and argue why for a good guiding function the clip is essentially
  never reached.  Modify the program to count how often it is, at
  $\alpha=1.00$, $\beta=0.40$ and at $\alpha=0.80$, $\beta=0.10$.

\item[c)] Switch off the population control by setting the feedback $\kappa$
  in Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-populationcontrol}) to zero, and run the program.  Plot
  the number of walkers against the step number.  What kind of stochastic
  process does it perform, and what happens to it eventually?

\item[d)] Raise $\kappa$ to $1$ instead.  The population is now held very
  tightly.  Show that the energy is nevertheless \emph{worse}, and explain why
  in terms of the fluctuations that $E_T$ injects into
  Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-branching}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exercise 3: the time step}
\label{ex:15-timestep}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 3: the time step}

\begin{enumerate}
\item[a)] Remove the Metropolis-Hastings accept/reject test from the
  drift-diffusion step of \texttt{dmc.py}, so that every proposed move is
  taken.  Repeat Table~\ref{tab:15-timestep}.  By how much does the time-step
  bias grow, and why is the extrapolated value nevertheless still correct?

\item[b)] Reproduce Table~\ref{tab:15-timestep} and fit the energies both to
  $E(\Delta\tau)=E(0)+c_1\Delta\tau$ and to
  $E(0)+c_1\Delta\tau+c_2\Delta\tau^2$.  Compare the two intercepts and their
  $\chi^2$.  Which fit is justified by the data, and what does that say about
  the order of the Trotter error in Eq.~(\ref{eq:15-trotter})?

\item[c)] The correlation time in Table~\ref{tab:15-timestep} grows as
  $\Delta\tau$ shrinks.  Show that the product
  $\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}\Delta\tau$ is roughly constant, and draw the
  consequence for how the number of steps should be chosen when the time step
  is reduced.

\item[d)] Estimate the total computer time needed to reach a statistical error
  of $10^{-5}$ on the extrapolated energy, with the good guiding function and
  with the poor one.  Which is the better investment, and by what factor?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exercise 4: nodes}
\label{ex:15-nodes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 4: nodes}

\begin{enumerate}
\item[a)] Explain why the trial function of Eq.~(\ref{eq:13-trial}) is
  nodeless, and why this follows from the two electrons being in a spin
  singlet rather than being an accident of the functional form.

\item[b)] Write down a trial function for the lowest \emph{triplet} state of
  the same dot, in which the spatial part must be antisymmetric.  Where are
  its nodes?  Show that they are fixed by symmetry alone and are therefore
  exact, so that a fixed-node calculation of this state is exact as well.

\item[c)] Implement the triplet in \texttt{dmc.py}: multiply the trial
  function by the antisymmetric factor found in b), reject any move that
  changes its sign, and run.  Compare with the variational energy of the same
  trial function.

\item[d)] For the six-electron dot of Chapter~\ref{chap:applications} the
  nodes come from a Slater determinant of oscillator orbitals and are
  \emph{not} exact.  Explain why the fixed-node energy is nevertheless an
  upper bound, and why it cannot be improved by increasing the number of
  walkers, reducing the time step, or running for longer.
\end{enumerate}
