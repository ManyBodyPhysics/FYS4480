\chapter{Elements from statistics}
\label{chap:statelements}
%=============================================================================

Every method so far has expanded the wave function in a basis.  Hartree-Fock
picked the best single determinant in that basis, perturbation theory and
coupled cluster added determinants systematically, and full configuration
interaction used all of them.  The error was always a truncation -- of the
cluster operator, of the perturbation series, of the single-particle space --
and Chapter~\ref{chap:applications} showed that for a realistic system the
last of these can be the largest.

The stochastic methods of the coming chapters do something different.  They
never write down a basis at all.  A variational Monte Carlo calculation takes
a trial wave function, evaluates it at points drawn at random from
$|\Psi_T|^2$, and averages; a diffusion Monte Carlo calculation lets an
ensemble of walkers evolve under an equation that is the Schr\"odinger
equation in imaginary time.  Neither has a basis-set error.  What they have
instead is a \emph{statistical} error, and controlling it needs a set of tools
that has not appeared in this book yet.

That is what this chapter is for.  We need probability distributions and their
moments, the central limit theorem and the $1/\sqrt{N}$ law that follows from
it, the correlation time that tells us how many of our samples were really
independent, random walks and their relation to the diffusion equation,
Markov chains and detailed balance, and finally the Metropolis algorithm --
which is how one samples a distribution that can only be evaluated up to an
unknown constant, the situation every many-body problem presents.

The examples are all Python and all in \texttt{montecarlo.py}, and wherever it
is possible the running example is the quantum dot of
Chapter~\ref{chap:applications}.  The chapter ends with a first variational
Monte Carlo calculation on that system.  The check that ties the two chapters
together is worth stating in advance: with the variational parameter set to
one, the trial function of Section~\ref{sec:firstvmc} \emph{is} the
minimal-basis Hartree-Fock determinant, so the Monte Carlo energy must come
out at $3.25331414$, the first row of Table~\ref{tab:11-hfconvergence}.  It
does.

\section{Stochastic variables and probability distributions}
\label{sec:pdfs}

\index{stochastic variable}\index{probability distribution function}
A \emph{stochastic variable} is characterised by two things: a \emph{domain}
$\mathbb{D}=\{x\}$ of the values it can take, and a \emph{probability
distribution function} (PDF) $p(x)$ on that domain.  We write stochastic
variables as capitals, $X,Y,\dots$, and their values as lower case.

For a discrete domain the PDF is a probability outright,
$p(x)=\mathrm{Prob}(X=x)$; the tossing of two dice has the domain
$\{2,3,\dots,12\}$ and the PDF $\{1/36,2/36,\dots,1/36\}$.  For a continuous
domain $p(x)$ is a \emph{density}: the probability of finding $X$ in an
infinitesimal interval about $x$ is $p(x)\,dx$, and
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Prob}(a\le X\le b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx .
  \label{eq:12-probinterval}
\end{equation}
The \emph{cumulative distribution function} (CDF) is
\begin{equation}
  P(x) = \mathrm{Prob}(X\le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x}p(x')\,dx',
  \qquad p(x) = \frac{d}{dx}P(x),
  \label{eq:12-cdf}
\end{equation}
and it will turn out to be the single most useful object in the chapter: it is
what converts one distribution into another.

Every PDF must be positive and normalised,
\begin{equation}
  p(x)\ge0,
  \qquad
  \sum_{x_i\in\mathbb{D}}p(x_i) = 1
  \quad\text{or}\quad
  \int_{\mathbb{D}}p(x)\,dx = 1 .
  \label{eq:12-normalisation}
\end{equation}
Three continuous distributions will be used throughout: the uniform
distribution on $[a,b]$, $p(x)=1/(b-a)$; the exponential,
$p(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ on $[0,\infty)$, which is the PDF of
radioactive decay; and the Gaussian or normal distribution
\begin{equation}
  p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
    \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
  \label{eq:12-gaussian}
\end{equation}
which the central limit theorem will single out.

\paragraph{Expectation values and moments.}
For any function $h$ on the domain,
\begin{equation}
  \mean{h} = \int h(x)p(x)\,dx .
  \label{eq:12-expectation}
\end{equation}
Taking $h(x)=x^n$ gives the \emph{moments} $\mean{x^n}$.  The zeroth moment is
the normalisation, the first is the \emph{mean} $\mu=\mean{x}$, and the second
\emph{central} moment is the \emph{variance},
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^2 = \mean{(x-\mean{x})^2} = \mean{x^2}-\mean{x}^2 ,
  \label{eq:12-variance}
\end{equation}
whose square root $\sigma$ is the standard deviation, the ``spread'' of $p$
about its mean.  Two PDFs are equal if and only if all their moments are.

\paragraph{Transforming a distribution.}
Let $Y=h(X)$ with $h$ strictly monotonic, so that $h^{-1}$ exists.  Then
$P_Y(y)=P_X(h^{-1}(y))$ for increasing $h$, and differentiating,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  p_Y(y) = p_X\!\left(h^{-1}(y)\right)
    \left|\frac{d}{dy}h^{-1}(y)\right| \;}
  \label{eq:12-transform}
\end{equation}
This is the formula that turns a uniform random number generator into a
generator for anything we like, and Section~\ref{sec:mcintegration} uses it
repeatedly.

\paragraph{Several variables, and independence.}
A PDF of one variable is \emph{univariate}, of several \emph{multivariate}.
The variables $\{X_i\}$ are \emph{independent} -- equivalently
\emph{uncorrelated} -- when the joint PDF factorises,
\begin{equation}
  P(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}p_i(x_i).
  \label{eq:12-independence}
\end{equation}
Qualitatively: the events tell each other nothing.  Independence is what makes
the next two sections work, and its failure is what
Section~\ref{sec:correlations} is about.

\section{The central limit theorem and the square-root law}
\label{sec:clt}

\index{central limit theorem}
Suppose we draw $m$ values from a PDF $p(x)$ with mean $\mu$ and variance
$\sigma^2$ and form their average $z=(x_1+\dots+x_m)/m$.  What is the PDF of
$z$?  Writing the constraint as a $\delta$-function and using its integral
representation,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{p}(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dq\,
    e^{\imath q(z-\mu)}
    \left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dx\,p(x)\,
      e^{\imath q(\mu-x)/m}\right]^m .
  \label{eq:12-cltintegral}
\end{equation}
Expanding the inner integral, the term linear in $(\mu-x)$ vanishes because
$\mu$ is the mean, and what is left is
\begin{equation}
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dx\,p(x)\,e^{\imath q(\mu-x)/m}
   = 1 - \frac{q^2\sigma^2}{2m^2} + \cdots ,
  \label{eq:12-cltexpansion}
\end{equation}
so that in the limit $m\to\infty$
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \tilde{p}(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,(\sigma/\sqrt{m})}
    \exp\left(-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2(\sigma/\sqrt{m})^2}\right) \;}
  \label{eq:12-clt}
\end{equation}
The average of $m$ samples from \emph{any} distribution with a finite
variance is normally distributed, with the same mean and with variance
$\sigma^2/m$.  Nothing about $p$ survives except its first two moments.

Two consequences run through everything that follows.  First, the error on a
Monte Carlo average is
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_m = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{m}}
  \qquad\text{or, for a measured variance,}\qquad
  \sigma_m \approx \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{m-1}} ,
  \label{eq:12-sqrtlaw}
\end{equation}
independent of the dimensionality of the problem.  A deterministic quadrature
on a grid needs $N^d$ points to reach a given accuracy in $d$ dimensions;
Monte Carlo needs $N$, whatever $d$ is.  That is the entire reason for using
it on a many-body system.  Second, the convergence is slow: a factor of ten in
accuracy costs a factor of a hundred in samples.  Everything called
``variance reduction'' is an attempt to shrink $\sigma$ rather than to
increase $m$.

distribution imaginable, the uniform one.

\section{Correlated data and the true error}
\label{sec:correlations}

\index{autocorrelation time}\index{blocking}
Equation~(\ref{eq:12-sqrtlaw}) assumed independent samples.  A Markov chain
does not produce them: consecutive configurations of a Metropolis walk are
similar by construction, and using $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ then \emph{understates}
the error.  This is the one mistake that makes a Monte Carlo result look
better than it is, and it is worth setting up properly.

Writing the sample variance of $mn$ measurements out in full,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_m^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}
   + \frac{2}{mn^2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\sum_{k<l}^{n}
     \left(x_{\alpha,k}-\mean{X_m}\right)
     \left(x_{\alpha,l}-\mean{X_m}\right),
  \label{eq:12-variancesplit}
\end{equation}
the first term is what the central limit theorem gives and the second is the
covariance, which vanishes only if the measurements are independent.
Collecting the covariance into partial sums over the separation $d$,
\begin{equation}
  f_d = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n-d}
    \left(x_{\alpha,k}-\mean{X_m}\right)
    \left(x_{\alpha,k+d}-\mean{X_m}\right),
  \qquad
  \kappa_d = \frac{f_d}{\sigma^2},
  \label{eq:12-autocorrelation}
\end{equation}
defines the \emph{autocorrelation function} $\kappa_d$, which starts at
$\kappa_0=1$ and decays.  The variance becomes
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \sigma_m^2 = \frac{\tau}{n}\,\sigma^2 ,
  \qquad
  \tau = 1 + 2\sum_{d=1}^{n-1}\kappa_d \;}
  \label{eq:12-tau}
\end{equation}
with $\tau$ the \emph{autocorrelation time}.  For independent data $\tau=1$.
Otherwise the effect of correlation is exactly a reduction of the number of
measurements,
\begin{equation}
  n_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{n}{\tau},
  \label{eq:12-neff}
\end{equation}
and the honest error bar is $\sqrt{\tau}$ times the naive one.

How much this matters is easy to underestimate.  Sampling a unit Gaussian by
Metropolis with a step of $0.5$ -- a perfectly reasonable-looking choice, with
an acceptance rate of $90\%$ -- gives $\tau=54.5$ over $100\,000$ samples.
The naive error is $0.0031$ and the true one $0.0231$, a factor of seven, and
the $100\,000$ measurements were worth $1836$ independent ones.

\paragraph{Blocking.}
Computing $\tau$ requires the whole time series and a decision about where to
truncate the sum, which is awkward.  The \emph{blocking} method of Flyvbjerg
and Petersen avoids both.  Average neighbouring pairs of measurements to
produce a shorter series, compute the naive error of that, and repeat.  The
naive error grows as the blocks get longer and correlations are averaged
away, and then plateaus; the plateau is the true error.  For the chain above
the naive error climbs from $0.0031$ through $0.0085$ and $0.0181$ to a
plateau near $0.022$, which is the corrected error found from $\tau$.  No
autocorrelation function, and no truncation decision.

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{The practical rule.} Always report an error obtained either from the
autocorrelation time or from blocking.  A naive $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ from a
Markov chain is not a small underestimate; in the example above it is wrong
by a factor of seven, and in a poorly tuned calculation it can be wrong by
orders of magnitude.
\end{notebox}

\section{Random numbers}
\label{sec:randomnumbers}

\index{random number generator}
Everything rests on a supply of numbers uniformly distributed on $[0,1)$.  Real
generators are deterministic algorithms -- hence \emph{pseudo}-random -- and
the classic construction is the linear congruential generator
\begin{equation}
  x_{n+1} = \left(a\,x_n + c\right)\bmod M ,
  \label{eq:12-lcg}
\end{equation}
which is fast, has period at most $M$, and can be very bad.  The instructive
failure is RANDU, with $a=65539$, $c=0$ and $M=2^{31}$, once shipped with
IBM's scientific library.  It is uniform, and its consecutive values are
uncorrelated at lag one.  But it satisfies
\begin{equation}
  x_{n+2} = 6x_{n+1} - 9x_n \pmod{1}
  \label{eq:12-randu}
\end{equation}
\emph{exactly}, so every triple of consecutive numbers lies on one of fifteen
planes in the unit cube.  A one-dimensional integral sampled with RANDU is
fine; a three-dimensional one is systematically wrong.
Table~\ref{tab:12-randu} shows the two tests that pass and the one that
fails.

\section{Monte Carlo integration and importance sampling}
\label{sec:mcintegration}

\index{Monte Carlo integration}\index{importance sampling}
The crudest scheme takes
\begin{equation}
  I = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx = \mean{f}
   \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x_i),
  \qquad x_i \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1),
  \label{eq:12-bruteforce}
\end{equation}
with the error $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-sqrtlaw}).  Applied to
$\int_0^1 4/(1+x^2)\,dx=\pi$ the product $\text{error}\times\sqrt{N}$ stays at
$0.64$ from $N=10^3$ to $N=10^6$, which is the square-root law in the most
direct form available.

\paragraph{Changing variables.}
Random numbers arrive uniform on $[0,1)$; integrals rarely do.  Conserving
probability, $p(y)\,dy = p(x)\,dx = dx$, and integrating,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\; x = P(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{y}p(y')\,dy' \;}
  \label{eq:12-inversecdf}
\end{equation}
so that $y=P^{-1}(x)$ generates $p$ from a uniform $x$.  Two examples.  The
uniform distribution on $[a,b]$ gives $y=a+(b-a)x$.  The exponential
$p(y)=\lambda e^{-\lambda y}$ has $P(y)=1-e^{-\lambda y}$ and therefore
\begin{equation}
  y = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(1-x),
  \label{eq:12-exponentialsample}
\end{equation}
which \texttt{montecarlo.py} confirms has mean and variance $1$ at
$\lambda=1$.  The Gaussian CDF cannot be inverted in closed form, and the
standard trick is to do two dimensions at once: with $u_1,u_2$ uniform,
\begin{equation}
  y = \sqrt{-2\ln(1-u_1)}\,\cos(2\pi u_2)
  \label{eq:12-boxmuller}
\end{equation}
is normally distributed with zero mean and unit variance.  This is the
Box-Muller transformation, and it is Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-transform}) applied to
the polar form of the two-dimensional Gaussian.

\paragraph{Acceptance-rejection.}
When even that fails there is von Neumann's method: bound $p$ by $M$ on
$[a,b]$, draw $x$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ and $s$ uniformly on $[0,M]$, and
accept $x$ if $s<p(x)$.  It needs nothing but the ability to evaluate $p$, and
it wastes a fraction $1-\bar{p}/M$ of the samples.  Applied to
$\int_0^3 e^x\,dx$ it gives $19.0375\pm0.0626$ against the exact $19.0855$.

\paragraph{Importance sampling.}
The real gain comes from choosing what to sample.  If $p$ is a normalised PDF
resembling the integrand,
\begin{equation}
  I = \int_a^b F(y)\,dy = \int_a^b p(y)\,\frac{F(y)}{p(y)}\,dy
   \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{F(y_i)}{p(y_i)},
  \qquad y_i\sim p ,
  \label{eq:12-importance}
\end{equation}
and the variance is that of $F/p$ rather than of $F$.  If $p$ follows $F$
closely the ratio is nearly constant and the variance nearly vanishes.  The
conditions on $p$ are that it be normalisable and positive, analytically
integrable, and that its integral be invertible so that
Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-inversecdf}) can be used.

The six-dimensional integral
\begin{equation}
  I = \int d^3x\,d^3y\;e^{-x^2-y^2}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right)^2
    = 3\pi^3 = 93.018830\ldots
  \label{eq:12-sixdim}
\end{equation}
makes the point.  Sampling uniformly in a box wastes almost every point on the
exponential tails.  Writing $e^{-x^2}=\sqrt{\pi}$ times a normal density of
variance $\tfrac12$ and sampling that instead leaves only the polynomial
$(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^2$ to average.  Table~\ref{tab:12-sixdim} compares the two.

\setlength{\tabcolsep}{14pt}

\paragraph{Why quantum mechanics forces this on us.}
The expectation value of a Hamiltonian in a trial state can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
  \mean{H}
   = \int dx\,\Psi^{*}(x)\hat{H}\Psi(x)
   = \int dx\,\left|\Psi(x)\right|^2\,
     \frac{\hat{H}\Psi(x)}{\Psi(x)}
   = \int dx\,p(x)\,E_L(x),
  \label{eq:12-localenergy}
\end{equation}
with $p=|\Psi|^2$ the quantum-mechanical probability density and
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;E_L(x) = \frac{\hat{H}\Psi(x)}{\Psi(x)}\;}
  \label{eq:12-localenergydef}
\end{equation}
the \emph{local energy}.  This is importance sampling with the best possible
choice of $p$, and it has a remarkable property.  If $\Psi$ is an exact
eigenfunction then $E_L$ is the constant $E$ everywhere, the average is $E$
with \emph{zero variance}, and a single sample suffices.  For an approximate
$\Psi$ the local energy is smooth near the exact solution and the variance is
small.  The whole of variational Monte Carlo is contained in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-localenergy}), and the variance of $E_L$ is a direct measure
of how good the trial function is -- often a more sensitive one than the
energy itself.

\section{Random walks and the diffusion equation}
\label{sec:randomwalks}

\index{random walk}\index{diffusion equation}
Sampling $|\Psi|^2$ for a many-body system cannot be done by inverting a
cumulative distribution: we do not know the normalisation, and in $3N$
dimensions we could not invert it if we did.  What is done instead is to build
a random walk whose stationary distribution is the one we want.  This section
sets up the machinery; the next turns it into an algorithm.

A walker on a line takes steps $\pm l$ with equal probability.  After $n$
steps
\begin{equation}
  \mean{x} = 0,
  \qquad
  \mean{x^2} = n\,l^2 ,
  \label{eq:12-walkmoments}
\end{equation}
so the walker spreads as $\sqrt{t}$ rather than $t$: this is diffusion, not
transport.  Simulating $4000$ walkers reproduces
$\mean{x^2}/n = 1.00$ to two decimals at every $n$ from $25$ to $400$.

That this really is diffusion can be shown exactly.  Fick's law states that
the particle flux is proportional to the gradient of the density,
$j=-D\,\partial w/\partial x$, and combining it with the continuity equation
gives the diffusion equation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial w(x,t)}{\partial t}
   = D\,\frac{\partial^2 w(x,t)}{\partial x^2}.
  \label{eq:12-diffusion}
\end{equation}
Now discretise the walk instead.  With $x=il$, $t=n\epsilon$ and the
transition probability
\begin{equation}
  W_{ij} = \begin{cases}\tfrac12 & |i-j|=1\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
  \label{eq:12-walktransition}
\end{equation}
the distribution after one step obeys $w_i(t+\epsilon)=\sum_j W_{ij}w_j(t)$,
that is
\begin{equation}
  w(x,t+\epsilon) = \tfrac12 w(x+l,t) + \tfrac12 w(x-l,t).
  \label{eq:12-walkrecursion}
\end{equation}
Subtracting $w(x,t)$ from both sides and multiplying by $l^2/\epsilon$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{w(x,t+\epsilon)-w(x,t)}{\epsilon}
   = \frac{l^2}{2\epsilon}\,
     \frac{w(x+l,t)-2w(x,t)+w(x-l,t)}{l^2},
  \label{eq:12-walktodiffusion}
\end{equation}
which is precisely the discretised diffusion
equation~(\ref{eq:12-diffusion}) with
\begin{equation}
  D = \frac{l^2}{2\epsilon}.
  \label{eq:12-diffusioncoefficient}
\end{equation}
A random walk \emph{is} the diffusion equation, and iterating the transition
matrix \emph{is} solving it.  Starting all walkers at the origin, the exact
solution of the discrete problem is the binomial distribution
\begin{equation}
  w_i(n\epsilon) = \frac{1}{2^n}\binom{n}{\tfrac12(n+i)},
  \qquad |i|\le n ,
  \label{eq:12-binomial}
\end{equation}
which by the central limit theorem tends to a Gaussian of variance $2Dt$.
This connection is the reason diffusion Monte Carlo works at all: the
Schr\"odinger equation in imaginary time is a diffusion equation, and it can
therefore be solved by letting walkers diffuse.

\paragraph{Equilibrium and entropy.}
Left on an infinite line the walkers spread for ever.  On a finite ring they
cannot, and what happens instead is worth watching.  Define the entropy of the
walker distribution,
\begin{equation}
  S = -\sum_i w_i\ln w_i ,
  \label{eq:12-entropy}
\end{equation}
which is zero when all walkers sit on one site and $\ln N_{\mathrm{sites}}$
when they are spread evenly.  For $4000$ walkers on $101$ sites with periodic
boundaries, $S$ rises from $0.69$ after one step through $3.05$ after a
hundred to $4.55$ after three thousand, approaching the uniform value
$\ln 101 = 4.6151$ and then staying there.

The system has reached a steady state, and nothing in the dynamics put it
there.  It happened because the walk can reach every site from every other,
given enough time.  That property is called \emph{ergodicity}, and it is the
first of the two conditions the next section will impose.

\section{Markov chains, detailed balance and the Metropolis algorithm}
\label{sec:metropolis}

\index{Markov chain}\index{detailed balance}\index{Metropolis algorithm}
A \emph{Markov process} is a random walk in which the next state depends only
on the present one.  Written as a matrix acting on the probability vector,
\begin{equation}
  w_i(t+1) = \sum_j W_{ij}\,w_j(t),
  \qquad \sum_i W_{ij} = 1 ,
  \label{eq:12-markov}
\end{equation}
and iterating drives $w$ towards the eigenvector of $\bm{W}$ with eigenvalue
one -- the steady state.  For the four-state example
\begin{equation}
  \bm{W} = \begin{pmatrix}
    1/4 & 1/9 & 3/8 & 1/3\\
    2/4 & 2/9 & 0   & 1/3\\
    0   & 1/9 & 3/8 & 0\\
    1/4 & 5/9 & 2/8 & 1/3
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:12-examplematrix}
\end{equation}
starting from $\bm{w}=(1,0,0,0)$ the iteration converges in $22$ steps to
\[
  \bm{w}_{\infty} =
  (0.244318,\;0.319602,\;0.056818,\;0.379261),
\]
which is exactly the eigenvector, computed independently.  The starting point
is irrelevant: any initial distribution converges to the same steady state.

\paragraph{The master equation and detailed balance.}
In continuous time Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-markov}) becomes the master equation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dw_i(t)}{dt}
   = \sum_j\left[W(j\to i)w_j - W(i\to j)w_i\right],
  \label{eq:12-master}
\end{equation}
the rate in balanced against the rate out.  At the steady state the left-hand
side vanishes, but that alone does not guarantee convergence to the right
distribution -- cyclic solutions can satisfy it.  The stronger condition,
which does, is \emph{detailed balance}:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;W(j\to i)\,w_j = W(i\to j)\,w_i
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \frac{W(j\to i)}{W(i\to j)} = \frac{w_i}{w_j} \;}
  \label{eq:12-detailedbalance}
\end{equation}
Every pair of states exchanges probability at equal rates in both directions.
Note what has happened: only the \emph{ratio} $w_i/w_j$ appears.  The
normalisation of the target distribution has disappeared, and for a many-body
system that normalisation is exactly what we cannot compute.

\paragraph{The algorithm.}
Split the transition probability into a proposal and an acceptance,
$W(i\to j)=T(i\to j)A(i\to j)$.  If the proposal is symmetric,
$T(i\to j)=T(j\to i)$, detailed balance requires
\begin{equation}
  \frac{A(j\to i)}{A(i\to j)} = \frac{w_i}{w_j}.
  \label{eq:12-acceptanceratio}
\end{equation}
This fixes only the ratio.  To accept as many moves as possible we set the
larger of the two to one, giving the Metropolis choice
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  A(i\to j) = \min\left(1,\;\frac{w_j}{w_i}\right) \;}
  \label{eq:12-metropolis}
\end{equation}
A move to a more probable state is always accepted; a move to a less probable
one is accepted with probability $w_j/w_i$, tested against a uniform random
number.  The algorithm is then:

\begin{svgraybox}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Start from a configuration $x^{(0)}$.
\item Propose $x'$ from a symmetric distribution $T(x'|x^{(k)})$.
\item Accept, $x^{(k+1)}=x'$, with probability $\min(1,w(x')/w(x^{(k)}))$;
  otherwise set $x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}$ and keep the old configuration.
\item Discard an initial burn-in, then accumulate averages.
\end{enumerate}
\end{svgraybox}

\noindent
Rejected moves count: the old configuration is measured again.  Omitting it
biases the average.

\paragraph{Choosing the step.}
The proposal width is the one free parameter and it matters more than it
looks.  Table~\ref{tab:12-step} shows why.  A small step is almost always
accepted but moves nowhere; a large one is almost always rejected and the
walker stands still.  Both produce long correlation times and therefore large
error bars, and the optimum is in between.  The usual rule of thumb is to tune
for an acceptance rate near one half.

\section{Fokker-Planck and Langevin equations}
\label{sec:fokkerplanck}

\index{Fokker-Planck equation}\index{Langevin equation}
A symmetric proposal is wasteful: it proposes moves into regions where the
target density is negligible, and they are rejected.  The cure is to bias the
proposal towards the high-density regions, which requires knowing how a
diffusing particle behaves under a force.  That is the content of the
Fokker-Planck and Langevin equations, and it is the basis of the
importance-sampled Monte Carlo of the next chapters.

Consider a transition probability $W(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_0,t_0)$.  Composing two
successive transitions gives the Einstein-Smoluchowski-Kolmogorov-Chapman
relation
\begin{equation}
  W(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_0,t_0)
   = \int d\bm{x}'\,W(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}',t')\,W(\bm{x}',t'|\bm{x}_0,t_0),
  \label{eq:12-eskc}
\end{equation}
which simply says that the particle must be somewhere at the intermediate
time.  Expanding for a short time step in the moments
\begin{equation}
  M_n = \frac{1}{\tau}\int d\xi\,\xi^n\,W(\bm{x}+\xi,\tau|\bm{x})
      = \frac{\mean{[\Delta x(\tau)]^n}}{\tau},
  \label{eq:12-moments}
\end{equation}
and keeping only $n=1$ and $n=2$ -- higher moments vanish faster than $\tau$
for a diffusive process -- gives the \emph{Fokker-Planck equation}
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \frac{\partial W}{\partial t}
   = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(M_1 W\right)
   + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\left(M_2 W\right) \;}
  \label{eq:12-fokkerplanck}
\end{equation}
The first term is drift and the second diffusion.  With $M_2=2D$ constant and
$M_1$ a drift velocity the solution is a Gaussian, and it is that Gaussian
which will be used as the proposal distribution $T$.

The same physics from the particle's point of view is the \emph{Langevin
equation}.  A particle in a fluid feels a systematic drag proportional to its
velocity and a random force from the molecular collisions,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\bm{v}}{dt} = -\xi\bm{v} + \bm{F}(t).
  \label{eq:12-langevin}
\end{equation}
Requiring that the velocity distribution relax to the equipartition value
$\mean{v^2}=3kT/m$ fixes the strength of the random force,
\begin{equation}
  \mean{\bm{F}(t)\cdot\bm{F}(t')}
   = 6\,\frac{kT}{m}\,\xi\,\delta(t-t'),
  \label{eq:12-fluctuationdissipation}
\end{equation}
the \emph{fluctuation-dissipation theorem}: the same collisions that damp the
motion also drive it, and their magnitudes are not independent.  Integrating
the Langevin equation gives the mean square displacement, which for long times
becomes $\mean{(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_0)^2}=6kTt/m\xi$, and comparing with
$\mean{(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_0)^2}=6Dt$ gives the Einstein relation
\begin{equation}
  D = \frac{kT}{m\xi}.
  \label{eq:12-einstein}
\end{equation}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Where this is going.} In a quantum Monte Carlo calculation the role of
the drift is played by the \emph{quantum force}
$\bm{F}=2\nabla\Psi_T/\Psi_T$, which pushes the walkers towards regions where
the trial function is large.  Proposing moves from the Fokker-Planck Green's
function rather than uniformly, and correcting the acceptance ratio for the
resulting asymmetry of $T$ -- the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm -- raises the
acceptance rate and shortens the correlation time substantially.  The same
equation, with the drift supplied by the trial function and the diffusion by
the kinetic energy, is what propagates the walkers in diffusion Monte Carlo.
\end{notebox}

\section{A first variational Monte Carlo calculation}
\label{sec:firstvmc}

\index{variational Monte Carlo}
We can now put all of it to work on the two-electron quantum dot of
Chapter~\ref{chap:applications}.  Take the trial function
\begin{equation}
  \Psi_T(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2;\alpha,\beta)
   = \exp\left(-\frac{\alpha\omega}{2}
       \left(r_1^2+r_2^2\right)\right)\,
     \exp\left(\frac{a\,r_{12}}{1+\beta r_{12}}\right),
  \label{eq:12-trialfunction}
\end{equation}
the first factor a scaled oscillator orbital for each electron and the second
a Jastrow factor with $a=1$ fixed by the Coulomb cusp condition for two
electrons of opposite spin in two dimensions.  The energy is the average of
the local energy~(\ref{eq:12-localenergydef}) over $|\Psi_T|^2$, sampled by
Metropolis.

\paragraph{The check.}
Switch the Jastrow factor off and set $\alpha=1$.  The trial function is then
the product of the two lowest oscillator orbitals, which is exactly the
minimal-basis Hartree-Fock determinant of Chapter~\ref{chap:applications}, and
the local energy collapses to
\begin{equation}
  E_L = 2\hbar\omega + \frac{1}{r_{12}} .
  \label{eq:12-localenergyalphaone}
\end{equation}
The average must therefore be
$2\hbar\omega+\sqrt{\pi/2}\sqrt{\hbar\omega}=3.253314$, the first row of
Table~\ref{tab:11-hfconvergence}.  Two hundred thousand samples give
$3.240689\pm0.009144$, which is $1.4$ standard errors away.  The two chapters
agree.

\paragraph{Varying $\alpha$.}
Still without the Jastrow factor, the averages can be done analytically as
well.  Under $|\Psi_T|^2\propto\exp(-\alpha\omega(r_1^2+r_2^2))$ one has
$\mean{r_1^2+r_2^2}=2/\alpha\omega$, and scaling lengths gives
$\mean{1/r_{12}}=\sqrt{\pi/2}\sqrt{\alpha\omega}$, so
\begin{equation}
  E(\alpha) = \omega\alpha + \frac{\omega}{\alpha}
   + \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sqrt{\alpha\omega},
  \label{eq:12-alphaenergy}
\end{equation}
with a minimum at $\alpha=0.7631$ where $E=3.168384$.  Every Monte Carlo
number can thus be checked against a formula, which is what
Table~\ref{tab:12-vmc} does.

cancel against the kinetic term, so the local energy stays finite as

Two things are worth drawing out.  The variance has fallen from about $3$ to
about $0.002$ -- three orders of magnitude -- and it is telling us something
the energy cannot.  Both quantities are second order in the error
$\delta=\Psi_T-\Psi_0$: writing
$E_L-E_0=(\hat{H}-E_0)\delta/\Psi_T=\bigO(\delta)$ shows that the variance is
$\bigO(\delta^2)$, and the variational principle makes the energy error
$\bigO(\delta^2)$ as well.  The difference is that we know what the variance
should be.  It is exactly zero for the exact wave function, so it is an
\emph{absolute} measure of quality, whereas the energy can only be compared
with an $E_0$ we do not have.  For that reason minimising the variance rather
than the energy is a standard optimisation strategy, and often a more stable
one, since the target value is known in advance.

And the energy itself, $3.000298\pm0.000365$, is below the CCSD result of
$3.013626$ from Chapter~\ref{chap:applications}.  That is not because the
many-body treatment is better -- CCSD is exact for two electrons in its basis.
It is because there is no basis here at all.  The Jastrow factor depends on
$r_{12}$ directly and reproduces the Coulomb cusp exactly, which no finite sum
of oscillator products can do.  The basis-set error that dominated
Chapter~\ref{chap:applications} has simply been eliminated, and replaced by a
statistical error that we know how to estimate and can reduce by running
longer.

That trade is the subject of the chapters that follow.

\subsection*{Exercise 1: distributions and their moments}
\label{ex:12-moments}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 1: distributions and moments}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] Compute the mean and variance of the uniform distribution on
  $[a,b]$ and of the exponential distribution with rate $\lambda$.
  \item[b)] Use Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-transform}) to find the PDF of $Y=X^2$ when
  $X$ is uniform on $[0,1]$.
  \item[c)] Show that the Cauchy distribution
  $p(x)=1/[\pi(1+x^2)]$ has no finite variance, and say what that does to the
  central limit theorem.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer to a).}
For the uniform distribution, $\mean{x}=(a+b)/2$ and
$\mean{x^2}=(b^3-a^3)/3(b-a)$, so
$\sigma^2=\mean{x^2}-\mean{x}^2=(b-a)^2/12$.  On $[0,1]$ this is $1/12$, the
value used in Table~\ref{tab:12-clt}.  For the exponential,
$\mean{x}=1/\lambda$ and $\mean{x^2}=2/\lambda^2$, so $\sigma^2=1/\lambda^2$:
mean and standard deviation coincide, which is why radioactive-decay
statistics are so noisy.

\paragraph{Answer to b).}
With $y=h(x)=x^2$ and $h^{-1}(y)=\sqrt{y}$ on $[0,1]$,
$|dh^{-1}/dy|=1/2\sqrt{y}$, and since $p_X=1$,
\[
  p_Y(y) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}},\qquad 0\le y\le 1 .
\]
It is normalised, and it diverges at the origin -- squaring a uniform variable
piles the values up near zero.  This is the mechanism by which the
inverse-CDF method works, run backwards.

\paragraph{Answer to c).}
$\mean{x^2}=\int x^2/[\pi(1+x^2)]\,dx$ diverges, since the integrand tends to
$1/\pi$ at large $|x|$.  The central limit theorem does not apply: averages of
$m$ Cauchy variables are Cauchy distributed with the \emph{same} width,
independently of $m$, so the error does not fall as $1/\sqrt{m}$ or at all.
Heavy tails are not an academic worry -- the $1/r_{12}$ tail in
Table~\ref{tab:12-vmc} is a mild version of the same problem, and it is what
makes those error bars unreliable.

\subsection*{Exercise 2: sampling and importance sampling}
\label{ex:12-sampling}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 2: sampling}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] Derive Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-exponentialsample}) and explain why
  $1-x$ may be replaced by $x$.
  \item[b)] Verify the Box-Muller transformation~(\ref{eq:12-boxmuller}) by
  transforming the two-dimensional Gaussian to polar coordinates.
  \item[c)] For $I=\int_0^1 e^{-x}\,dx$ compare the variance of brute-force
  sampling with importance sampling using $p(x)\propto e^{-x}$ on $[0,1]$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer to a).}
The CDF is $P(y)=\int_0^y\lambda e^{-\lambda y'}dy'=1-e^{-\lambda y}$.
Setting $x=P(y)$ and inverting gives $y=-\ln(1-x)/\lambda$.  Since $x$ is
uniform on $[0,1)$, so is $1-x$, and the two forms are statistically
identical; the version with $1-x$ is safer only because it avoids $\ln 0$
when the generator can return exactly zero.

\paragraph{Answer to b).}
For two independent standard normals the joint density is
$e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}/2\pi$.  In polar coordinates this is
$re^{-r^2/2}dr\,d\theta/2\pi$, which factorises: $\theta$ is uniform on
$[0,2\pi)$ and $r$ has CDF $1-e^{-r^2/2}$, invertible to
$r=\sqrt{-2\ln(1-u_1)}$.  Taking $x=r\cos\theta$ with $\theta=2\pi u_2$ gives
Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-boxmuller}), and $y=r\sin\theta$ gives a second, independent
normal variable for free.

\paragraph{Answer to c).}
Brute force averages $f=e^{-x}$ over the uniform distribution, giving
$\sigma^2=\mean{e^{-2x}}-\mean{e^{-x}}^2
=(1-e^{-2})/2-(1-e^{-1})^2\approx0.0332$.  With
$p(x)=e^{-x}/(1-e^{-1})$ the ratio $f/p=1-e^{-1}$ is \emph{constant}, so the
variance is exactly zero: the estimator returns the right answer from a single
sample.  This is the ideal case of Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-importance}), and it is the
same mechanism that makes the local energy constant for an exact
eigenfunction.

\subsection*{Exercise 3: Markov chains and detailed balance}
\label{ex:12-markov}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 3: Markov chains}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] Show that the columns of a transition matrix sum to one and that
  the steady state is the eigenvector with eigenvalue one.
  \item[b)] Show that detailed balance implies the steady-state condition,
  but not conversely.
  \item[c)] Verify that the Metropolis choice~(\ref{eq:12-metropolis})
  satisfies detailed balance for a symmetric proposal.
  \item[d)] What changes if the proposal is \emph{not} symmetric?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer to a).}
$\sum_i W_{ij}=1$ says that the walker, starting in $j$, ends up somewhere
with probability one.  A steady state satisfies $w=\bm{W}w$ by definition,
which is the eigenvalue equation with eigenvalue one.  Such an eigenvalue
always exists because $\bm{W}^{T}$ has the vector of ones as a right
eigenvector with eigenvalue one, and $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^{T}$ share
eigenvalues.

\paragraph{Answer to b).}
Summing detailed balance $W(j\to i)w_j=W(i\to j)w_i$ over $j$ gives
$\sum_j W(j\to i)w_j=w_i\sum_j W(i\to j)=w_i$, which is the steady-state
condition.  The converse fails: the steady-state condition constrains only the
\emph{net} flux into each state, so a cyclic flow
$1\to2\to3\to1$ with equal rates satisfies it while violating detailed balance
pairwise.  Detailed balance is the stronger and the safer requirement.

\paragraph{Answer to c).}
With $T$ symmetric, take $w_j>w_i$.  Then $A(i\to j)=1$ and
$A(j\to i)=w_i/w_j$, so
\[
  W(j\to i)w_j = T\,\frac{w_i}{w_j}\,w_j = T\,w_i
  = T\cdot1\cdot w_i = W(i\to j)w_i ,
\]
which is Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-detailedbalance}).  The case $w_j<w_i$ follows by
exchanging the labels, and $w_i=w_j$ is trivial.

\paragraph{Answer to d).}
The proposal no longer cancels and the acceptance ratio must compensate for
it,
\[
  A(i\to j) = \min\left(1,\,
    \frac{w_j\,T(j\to i)}{w_i\,T(i\to j)}\right),
\]
which is the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm.  This is exactly what is needed
when the proposal is drawn from the Fokker-Planck Green's function of
Section~\ref{sec:fokkerplanck}, since a drift towards high density is by
construction asymmetric.

\subsection*{Exercise 4: errors that are too good to be true}
\label{ex:12-errors}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Exercise 4: error estimates}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] Explain why $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ understates the error of a Markov
  chain, and in which direction the bias always goes.
  \item[b)] Show that blocking and the autocorrelation time measure the same
  thing.
  \item[c)] A calculation reports $E=3.0001\pm0.0001$ from a Metropolis run
  with an acceptance rate of $99\%$.  What would you check first?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Answer to a).}
Consecutive samples of a Markov chain are positively correlated, so the
covariance term in Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-variancesplit}) is positive and
$\tau=1+2\sum_d\kappa_d>1$.  The true variance is $\tau$ times the naive one,
so the naive error is always too \emph{small}: neglecting correlations never
makes a result look worse than it is, which is precisely why the mistake
survives.

\paragraph{Answer to b).}
Blocking averages $b$ consecutive measurements.  Once $b\gg\tau$ the blocks
are effectively independent, so the naive error of the blocked series is the
true error.  Quantitatively, the variance of a block mean is
$\tau\sigma^2/b$ for $b\gg\tau$, so the naive error of $n/b$ such blocks is
$\sqrt{\tau\sigma^2/n}$ -- Eq.~(\ref{eq:12-tau}) again.  The plateau in the
blocking curve is reached at $b\approx\tau$, so the curve measures the
correlation time as a by-product.

\paragraph{Answer to c).}
The acceptance rate.  Ninety-nine per cent means the proposal step is far too
small, the walker is crawling, and the correlation time is enormous -- in
Table~\ref{tab:12-step}, an acceptance of $98\%$ gave $\tau=585$, so the error
bar is understated by a factor of $\sqrt{585}\approx24$.  The quoted error is
almost certainly wrong by more than an order of magnitude.  Check $\tau$, or
block the data, and retune the step for an acceptance near one half.
